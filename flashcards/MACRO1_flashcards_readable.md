## Chapter 1: Aggregate Production and Prices (33 cards)


### ch01-001 — GDP definition · recall · D1

**Q:** What is gross domestic product (GDP)?

**Answer:** The market value of all final goods and services produced within a country during a specified period.

**Explanation:** Every word matters: market value lets unlike products be added; final avoids double counting; produced means current production; within a country makes GDP a location-based measure; and the period makes GDP a flow.


**Common trap:** Do not define GDP as everything bought or sold. Used goods and many financial transactions are not current production.


*Tags: definition, high-yield*


### ch01-002 — GDP definition · mcq · D1

**Q:** Which is the best definition of annual GDP?

**Answer:** The market value of all final goods and services produced within the country during the year.

**Explanation:** The course lecture explicitly uses this as a discrimination question. “Purchased” is too narrow because inventories can be produced without final sale; “bought and sold” incorrectly includes second-hand transactions.


**Common trap:** Watch for the words final, produced, within the country, and during the period.


**Choices:** A. Value of all goods and services bought and sold during the year | B. Value of all new goods and services produced during the year | C. Value of all final goods and services purchased during the year | D. Market value of all final goods and services produced within the country during the year
**Correct:** D


*Tags: mcq, definition*


### ch01-003 — GDP and second-hand goods · scenario · D1

**Q:** You sell a used textbook to another student. Is the sale included in current GDP?

**Answer:** No.

**Explanation:** The book was counted when it was newly produced. A resale merely transfers ownership of an existing asset and does not represent new production in the current period.


**Common trap:** A fee paid to a current-period intermediary for arranging a used-good sale could be current production, even though the used good itself is not.


*Tags: classification, GDP*


### ch01-004 — GDP and imports · scenario · D2

**Q:** An Australian household buys a newly produced book directly from the UK. How does it enter Australian GDP?

**Answer:** It is consumption and an import; the import subtraction offsets the consumption, so it does not add to Australian GDP.

**Explanation:** In \(Y = C + I + G + X - M\), the purchase may first appear in C, but because the good was produced abroad it also appears in M. Subtracting M ensures GDP measures domestic production.


**Common trap:** “Imports reduce GDP” is sloppy: the subtraction removes foreign production already embedded in C, I, or G.


*Tags: classification, net-exports*


### ch01-005 — Intermediate goods · recall · D2

**Q:** Why are intermediate goods not added separately to GDP?

**Answer:** To avoid double counting.

**Explanation:** Their value is embodied in the value of the final good. GDP can equivalently be calculated by summing value added at each stage of production.


**Common trap:** An intermediate good produced this period still contributes to GDP through the producer’s value added; it is just not counted twice.


*Tags: value-added, GDP*


### ch01-006 — Value added · formula · D2

**Q:** How do you calculate a firm’s value added?

**Answer:** Value added = value of sales/output - value of intermediate inputs purchased from other firms.

**Explanation:** Summing value added across all domestic producers gives the production-side measure of GDP. Wages, profits and other primary incomes are paid out of value added.


**Common trap:** Do not subtract wages as an intermediate input; labour is a factor of production, not an intermediate good.


*Tags: formula, value-added*


### ch01-007 — Expenditure GDP · formula · D1

**Q:** State the expenditure identity for GDP.

**Answer:** \(Y = C + I + G + NX = C + I + G + X - M\).

**Explanation:** C is household consumption, I is investment, G is government purchases of goods and services, and NX is exports minus imports. The identity is an accounting identity, not a behavioural theory.


**Common trap:** Transfers and government interest payments are not G because they are not purchases of newly produced final goods or services.


*Tags: formula, high-yield*


### ch01-008 — Consumption · classification · D2

**Q:** What belongs in C in the expenditure approach?

**Answer:** Household purchases of newly produced final goods and services, except new residential construction.

**Explanation:** Consumption includes durable and nondurable goods and services purchased by households. The course classifies new dwellings as investment, even though households buy them.


**Common trap:** A household buying shares or an existing house is not consumption expenditure on current production.


*Tags: GDP-components*


### ch01-009 — Investment · classification · D1

**Q:** What belongs in macroeconomic investment I?

**Answer:** Business fixed investment, new dwelling/residential construction, and inventory investment.

**Explanation:** Investment means production of new capital goods or additions to inventories, not the purchase of existing financial assets. Inventory investment is the change in inventory stocks over the period.


**Common trap:** Buying shares is saving/portfolio investment in ordinary language, but not I in the GDP expenditure identity.


*Tags: GDP-components, investment*


### ch01-010 — Inventory investment · calculation · D2

**Q:** Beginning inventories are 100 and end-of-period inventories are 75. What is inventory investment?

**Answer:** -25.

**Explanation:** Inventory investment is end stock minus beginning stock: 75 - 100 = -25. Negative inventory investment means inventories were run down during the period.


**Common trap:** Do not use the level of inventories as investment; use the change in the stock.


*Tags: calculation, investment*


### ch01-011 — Government spending · classification · D2

**Q:** Which government payments count as G in GDP?

**Answer:** Government purchases of newly produced goods and services.

**Explanation:** Government consumption and public investment count. Transfer payments and interest on government debt do not directly count because no current good or service is received in exchange.


**Common trap:** A transfer may later finance household C, but the transfer itself is not G.


*Tags: GDP-components, government*


### ch01-012 — Net exports · formula · D1

**Q:** How are net exports defined?

**Answer:** \(NX = X - M\).

**Explanation:** Exports are domestically produced goods and services purchased by foreigners and add to domestic production. Imports are foreign production and are subtracted to remove them from other expenditure categories.


**Common trap:** A trade deficit means NX < 0; it does not mean exports are zero.


*Tags: formula, international*


### ch01-013 — GDP vs GNI · recall · D2

**Q:** What is the core distinction between GDP and GNI?

**Answer:** GDP is based on where production occurs; GNI is based on income accruing to a country’s residents/owned factors of production.

**Explanation:** A foreign worker producing in Australia contributes to Australian GDP, but the income sent to the foreign-owned factor is removed when moving from GDP to GNI. Conversely, income earned by Australian-owned factors abroad is added.


**Common trap:** Nationality of the producer is not what determines GDP; location of production does.


*Tags: GDP, GNI*


### ch01-014 — Nominal vs real GDP · recall · D1

**Q:** What is the difference between nominal and real GDP?

**Answer:** Nominal GDP values current production at current prices; real GDP values production using a common set of prices so that changes reflect quantities rather than price changes.

**Explanation:** Nominal GDP can rise because quantities, prices, or both rose. Real GDP is the relevant measure for changes in aggregate production over time.


**Common trap:** Do not infer real growth from nominal growth without controlling for prices.


*Tags: nominal-real, GDP*


### ch01-015 — Real GDP · calculation · D2

**Q:** How do you calculate real GDP using a fixed base year?

**Answer:** Multiply each current-year quantity by its base-year price, then sum across goods and services.

**Explanation:** Using a common price vector isolates the quantity change. Different base years can give different real growth rates because relative prices change, which motivates chain weighting.


**Common trap:** The base year determines the prices, not the quantities.


*Tags: calculation, real-GDP*


### ch01-016 — Chain weighting · recall · D3

**Q:** Why does the course use chain-weighted real GDP growth?

**Answer:** Because growth measured with one fixed set of prices can depend heavily on the chosen base year; chain weighting averages adjacent-year growth measures using both years’ prices.

**Explanation:** The worksheet has you compute real growth with 2019 prices and with 2020 prices, then average the two. Chaining reduces base-year distortion and better reflects changing relative prices.


**Common trap:** Do not average nominal and real growth; average the two real growth rates constructed with adjacent price sets.


*Tags: real-GDP, chain-weighting*


### ch01-017 — GDP deflator · formula · D2

**Q:** How is the GDP price index/deflator related to nominal and real GDP?

**Answer:** GDP price index = nominal GDP / real GDP (often multiplied by 100).

**Explanation:** Rearranging gives real \(GDP=\frac{\text{nominal GDP}}{\text{price index}}\) when the index is written as a ratio rather than 100-based. The percentage change in the price index measures broad domestic-output inflation.


**Common trap:** Keep track of whether the index is 1.13 or 113; the economics is the same but arithmetic differs by a factor of 100.


*Tags: formula, price-level*


### ch01-018 — CPI definition · recall · D1

**Q:** What does the CPI measure?

**Answer:** The cost of a fixed representative basket of consumer goods and services in the current period relative to its cost in a base period.

**Explanation:** Fixing quantities is intended to isolate price changes. A common formula is \(CPI_t=\frac{\text{cost of base-year basket at current prices}}{\text{cost of base-year basket at base-year prices}}\), with or without multiplying by 100.


**Common trap:** The CPI basket is not the current year’s quantities when using the standard fixed-basket construction.


*Tags: CPI, definition*


### ch01-019 — Inflation · formula · D1

**Q:** How is the inflation rate calculated from a price index?

**Answer:** \(\pi_t = \frac{P_t-P_{t-1}}{P_{t-1}}\), usually expressed as a percentage.

**Explanation:** Inflation is the growth rate of the price level, not the price level itself. A falling but still positive inflation rate is disinflation; a negative inflation rate is deflation.


**Common trap:** A CPI of 120 does not mean inflation is 20% this year unless the previous CPI was 100.


*Tags: formula, inflation*


### ch01-020 — CPI vs GDP deflator · contrast · D3

**Q:** Give two important differences between the CPI and GDP deflator.

**Answer:** CPI uses a fixed consumer basket and can include imported consumer goods; the GDP deflator covers domestically produced final output and its weights change with current production.

**Explanation:** These measures can move differently when import prices change or when the composition of domestic production changes. CPI is designed as a cost-of-living measure for consumers; the GDP deflator is a price index for domestic output.


**Common trap:** Do not assume the two price indexes use the same basket or geographical coverage.


*Tags: CPI, GDP-deflator*


### ch01-021 — CPI bias · recall · D2

**Q:** What is substitution bias in a fixed-basket CPI?

**Answer:** The CPI can overstate the rise in the true cost of living because consumers substitute away from goods whose relative prices rise.

**Explanation:** A fixed basket assumes consumers keep buying the original quantities even when relative prices change. In reality, substitution can reduce the expenditure needed to maintain similar welfare.


**Common trap:** This is different from quality bias: substitution concerns quantities chosen in response to relative prices.


*Tags: CPI, bias*


### ch01-022 — CPI bias · recall · D2

**Q:** What is quality-change bias in the CPI?

**Answer:** If quality improves but the index treats the good as unchanged, measured price growth can overstate the rise in the true cost of living.

**Explanation:** The tutorial’s iPhone example keeps the sticker price constant while quality rises. A constant measured price then misses a fall in the quality-adjusted price.


**Common trap:** A higher price for a much better product is not necessarily pure inflation.


*Tags: CPI, bias*


### ch01-023 — GDP and welfare · recall · D2

**Q:** Why is real GDP per capita an imperfect measure of economic welfare?

**Answer:** It measures market production per person, not welfare directly.

**Explanation:** It omits or poorly captures household production, leisure, distribution, environmental degradation, many non-market activities and other dimensions of quality of life. It is still useful because material production strongly affects living standards.


**Common trap:** “GDP is imperfect” does not imply “GDP is useless.”


*Tags: welfare, GDP*


### ch01-024 — Growth rate · formula · D1

**Q:** What is the standard percentage growth formula for real GDP?

**Answer:** Growth = \(\frac{Y_t-Y_{t-1}}{Y_{t-1}} \times 100\%\).

**Explanation:** The denominator is the previous period’s level. The same percentage-change logic is used repeatedly in the course for GDP, prices, debt ratios and exchange-rate changes.


**Common trap:** Dividing by the new value instead of the old value is a common MCQ distractor.


*Tags: formula, growth*


### ch01-025 — Business cycle · recall · D2

**Q:** What is a classical business cycle recession?

**Answer:** A period in which the level of real GDP declines from a peak to a trough.

**Explanation:** The textbook distinguishes the classical cycle, which focuses on the level of GDP, from growth-cycle ideas that focus on deviations from trend or potential output.


**Common trap:** A low positive growth rate is not necessarily a classical recession if the level of GDP is still rising.


*Tags: business-cycle*


### ch01-026 — Technical recession · recall · D1

**Q:** What is the common “technical recession” rule?

**Answer:** Two consecutive quarters of negative real GDP growth.

**Explanation:** It is a convenient rule of thumb, not a complete economic definition of recession. Broader assessments can consider depth, duration and labour-market conditions.


**Common trap:** One negative quarter is not enough for this technical rule.


*Tags: business-cycle, definition*


### ch01-027 — Unexpected inflation · scenario · D1

**Q:** Who tends to gain from unexpectedly high inflation on a fixed nominal loan: borrower or lender?

**Answer:** The borrower gains and the lender loses in real terms.

**Explanation:** The nominal repayment is fixed, but unexpectedly higher prices reduce its purchasing power. The realized real interest rate is therefore lower than the lender expected.


**Common trap:** This result assumes the nominal rate was fixed before the inflation surprise.


*Tags: inflation, redistribution*


### ch01-028 — Unexpected inflation · recall · D2

**Q:** Why is volatile, unanticipated inflation costly even apart from redistribution?

**Answer:** It makes it harder to distinguish changes in relative prices from changes in the general price level and makes planning and contracts more uncertain.

**Explanation:** Relative-price signals guide resource allocation. Extra noise from unpredictable inflation can lead households and firms to make poorer decisions and increases forecasting and contracting costs.


**Common trap:** Perfectly anticipated inflation removes much of this uncertainty but not all inflation costs.


*Tags: inflation, costs*


### ch01-029 — Menu costs · recall · D1

**Q:** What are menu costs of inflation?

**Answer:** Real resource costs incurred because firms must change prices more frequently.

**Explanation:** The restaurant-menu example is literal, but the concept includes repricing systems, catalogues, contracts and administrative effort. They remain even when inflation is perfectly anticipated.


**Common trap:** Menu costs are not the loss of purchasing power itself.


*Tags: inflation, costs*


### ch01-030 — Shoe-leather costs · recall · D1

**Q:** What are shoe-leather costs of inflation?

**Answer:** Extra transaction costs caused by efforts to economise on non-interest-bearing or low-interest money balances.

**Explanation:** When inflation raises the opportunity cost of holding cash, people shift more often between money and interest-bearing assets. The classic metaphor is making more trips to the bank.


**Common trap:** They are not literally limited to worn-out shoes; they are resource costs of cash management.


*Tags: inflation, costs*


### ch01-031 — Bracket creep · scenario · D2

**Q:** What is bracket creep under an unindexed progressive income-tax system?

**Answer:** Inflation pushes nominal income into higher tax brackets even if real income has not increased.

**Explanation:** The tutorial shows a worker whose nominal income rises only to keep pace with prices, yet her tax liability rises faster because thresholds are fixed in nominal terms. Indexing thresholds to inflation prevents this arbitrary real tax increase.


**Common trap:** A higher nominal wage during inflation does not automatically mean a higher real wage.


*Tags: inflation, tax*


### ch01-032 — Positive inflation target · recall · D3

**Q:** Why might policymakers prefer a small positive inflation target rather than zero?

**Answer:** Positive inflation can facilitate real-wage adjustment when nominal wages are downward rigid and can leave more room to cut nominal interest rates before hitting a lower bound.

**Explanation:** The textbook uses both arguments. With positive inflation, real wages can fall even if money wages do not; and higher normal nominal rates give monetary policy more conventional easing space in downturns.


**Common trap:** This is an argument for low positive inflation, not for high or unstable inflation.


*Tags: inflation, policy*


### ch01-033 — Deflation · contrast · D3

**Q:** What is the difference between “good” and “bad” deflation in the textbook discussion?

**Answer:** Bad deflation is associated with weak output and high unemployment; good deflation can arise from widespread productivity/production gains that lower prices.

**Explanation:** The sign of inflation alone does not reveal the underlying cause. A supply-driven fall in prices can accompany rising real output, whereas demand-collapse deflation can reinforce stagnation.


**Common trap:** Do not infer recession solely from a negative inflation rate.


*Tags: deflation, business-cycle*


## Chapter 2: Employment, Unemployment and Labour Market (31 cards)


### ch02-001 — Labour-force classification · recall · D1

**Q:** What are the three mutually exclusive labour-market states for a working-age person?

**Answer:** Employed, unemployed, or not in the labour force.

**Explanation:** Labour-force statistics partition the working-age population into these three states. The labour force is the employed plus the unemployed; people outside it are neither employed nor actively counted as unemployed.


**Common trap:** “Not working” is not synonymous with unemployed.


*Tags: labour-force, definition*


### ch02-002 — Unemployment definition · recall · D1

**Q:** Under the standard labour-force definition, who counts as unemployed?

**Answer:** A working-age person without a job who is available for work and actively seeking work.

**Explanation:** The precise survey criteria matter because someone can want a job yet be outside the labour force if they are not actively searching. This is why discouraged workers are a recurring exam trap.


**Common trap:** Do not classify every person without paid work as unemployed.


*Tags: labour-force, definition*


### ch02-003 — Labour force · formula · D1

**Q:** How do you calculate the labour force?

**Answer:** Labour force \(LF=E+U\).

**Explanation:** The labour force excludes people classified as not in the labour force. It is the denominator of the unemployment rate and the numerator component of the participation rate.


**Common trap:** Do not use working-age population as the unemployment-rate denominator.


*Tags: formula, labour-force*


### ch02-004 — Unemployment rate · formula · D1

**Q:** How do you calculate the unemployment rate?

**Answer:** \(u = U / LF \times 100\%\).

**Explanation:** Only people in the labour force are in the denominator. A person moving from unemployment to not in the labour force can reduce the measured unemployment rate even without finding a job.


**Common trap:** Using U / working-age population gives a different statistic.


*Tags: formula, labour-force*


### ch02-005 — Participation rate · formula · D1

**Q:** How do you calculate the labour-force participation rate?

**Answer:** Participation rate = \(\frac{LF}{\text{working-age population}}\times100\%\).

**Explanation:** It measures the share of the working-age population either working or actively seeking work. Changes in participation can therefore change unemployment statistics even if employment is unchanged.


**Common trap:** Participation is not employment divided by working-age population.


*Tags: formula, labour-force*


### ch02-006 — Employment-to-population ratio · formula · D1

**Q:** How do you calculate the employment-to-population ratio?

**Answer:** Employment-to-population ratio = \(\frac{E}{\text{working-age population}}\times100\%\).

**Explanation:** This measure is less sensitive than the unemployment rate to whether non-employed people are actively searching. It gives a direct measure of the fraction of working-age people who are employed.


**Common trap:** Do not divide employment by the labour force; that would be 1 - unemployment rate.


*Tags: formula, labour-force*


### ch02-007 — Labour statistics · calculation · D2

**Q:** A country has working-age population 1,000, participation 70%, and employment-to-population 60%. Find LF, E, U and u.

**Answer:** \(LF = 700\); \(E = 600\); \(U = 100\); \(u = 100/700 = 14.29\%\).

**Explanation:** Participation gives \(LF = 0.70\times1000\). The employment ratio gives \(E = 0.60\times1000\). Unemployment is the residual within the labour force, then divide by LF for the rate.


**Common trap:** Do not subtract the employment rate from the participation rate and call the result the unemployment rate; that gives U as a share of population, not LF.


*Tags: calculation, labour-force*


### ch02-008 — Discouraged workers · scenario · D1

**Q:** How are discouraged workers normally classified in official labour-force statistics?

**Answer:** Not in the labour force.

**Explanation:** They do not have a job and may want one, but if they have stopped actively searching they fail the active-search criterion for unemployment. Their exit can make measured unemployment fall without labour-market improvement.


**Common trap:** Discouraged workers are not automatically counted as unemployed.


*Tags: classification, labour-force*


### ch02-009 — Frictional unemployment · recall · D1

**Q:** What is frictional unemployment?

**Answer:** Short-term unemployment arising from normal search and matching as workers move between jobs.

**Explanation:** Even a healthy labour market has search time because workers and jobs are heterogeneous and information is imperfect. A skilled worker interviewing for several suitable jobs is a typical course example.


**Common trap:** Frictional unemployment is not caused by an economy-wide shortage of jobs.


*Tags: unemployment-types*


### ch02-010 — Structural unemployment · recall · D1

**Q:** What is structural unemployment?

**Answer:** Unemployment caused by a mismatch between workers’ skills/location and available jobs or by persistent labour-market structures.

**Explanation:** Technological change, industry decline or geographic mismatch can leave workers unable to move quickly into available positions. It is part of the natural rate because it need not disappear at potential output.


**Common trap:** A temporary layoff caused by recession is cyclical, not structural.


*Tags: unemployment-types*


### ch02-011 — Cyclical unemployment · recall · D1

**Q:** What is cyclical unemployment?

**Answer:** Unemployment associated with short-run aggregate economic downturns and negative output gaps.

**Explanation:** When aggregate demand and output fall below potential, firms need fewer workers. This component rises in recessions and falls in booms.


**Common trap:** Cyclical unemployment is excluded from the natural unemployment rate.


*Tags: unemployment-types*


### ch02-012 — Natural unemployment · formula · D1

**Q:** How is the natural rate of unemployment conceptually decomposed?

**Answer:** Natural unemployment = frictional unemployment + structural unemployment.

**Explanation:** It is the unemployment consistent with normal utilisation of resources/potential output, not zero unemployment. Cyclical unemployment is the deviation from this natural benchmark.


**Common trap:** “Full employment” in this course does not mean \(u = 0\).


*Tags: unemployment-types, natural-rate*


### ch02-013 — Natural rate flows · formula · D3

**Q:** With constant labour force, job-separation rate s and job-finding rate f, what is the steady-state unemployment rate?

**Answer:** \(u^* = \frac{s}{s+f}\).

**Explanation:** In steady state, flows into unemployment equal flows out: \(sE = fU\). Using \(E = LF - U\) and dividing by LF yields \(u^* = \frac{s}{s+f}\). Higher finding rates lower the steady-state unemployment rate; higher separation rates raise it.


**Common trap:** Do not use s/f without the +s in the denominator.


*Tags: formula, natural-rate, flows*


### ch02-014 — Natural rate flows · calculation · D2

**Q:** If \(s = 0.02\) and \(f = 0.13\), what is the steady-state unemployment rate?

**Answer:** \(\frac{0.02}{0.02+0.13}=0.1333=13.33\%\).

**Explanation:** At that rate the number separating from jobs exactly equals the number finding jobs. This is the chapter review example and is a likely compact MCQ calculation.


**Common trap:** Rates must be in the same time units.


*Tags: calculation, natural-rate*


### ch02-015 — Labour-market flows · recall · D2

**Q:** How many possible directional flows are there among employment, unemployment and not-in-labour-force, excluding staying in the same state?

**Answer:** Six.

**Explanation:** Each of the three states can send people to either of the other two: E→U, E→N; U→E, U→N; N→E, N→U. Recognising these flows helps explain why unemployment can change through participation as well as hiring/firing.


**Common trap:** There are nine transitions if “stay put” transitions are included, but the question asks cross-state flows.


*Tags: labour-force, flows*


### ch02-016 — Potential output · recall · D1

**Q:** What is potential output Y*?

**Answer:** The level of aggregate production when labour and capital are utilised at normal, sustainable rates—roughly the economy’s long-run path.

**Explanation:** Potential output is not a hard physical maximum. Actual output can temporarily exceed Y* if resources are used above normal intensity, creating an expansionary output gap.


**Common trap:** Do not equate potential output with maximum possible output.


*Tags: potential-output, definition*


### ch02-017 — Output gap · formula · D1

**Q:** How is the percentage output gap defined?

**Answer:** Output gap = \(\frac{Y-Y^*}{Y^*}\times 100\%\).

**Explanation:** A negative gap means actual output is below potential; a positive gap means output is above potential. The course later links the gap to unemployment and inflation dynamics.


**Common trap:** The denominator is potential output Y*, not actual output Y.


*Tags: formula, output-gap*


### ch02-018 — Okun’s law · formula · D2

**Q:** State the course’s Okun’s-law relationship between the output gap and cyclical unemployment.

**Answer:** \(\frac{Y-Y^*}{Y^*}\times 100 = -\beta(u-u^*)\), with \(\beta>0\).

**Explanation:** A negative output gap corresponds to unemployment above its natural rate. The coefficient \(\beta\) measures how strongly output deviations are associated with cyclical unemployment.


**Common trap:** The minus sign matters: output and cyclical unemployment move in opposite directions.


*Tags: formula, Okun*


### ch02-019 — Okun’s law · calculation · D2

**Q:** If the output gap is -2%, \(\beta = 2.5\) and \(u^* = 4\%\), what is u?

**Answer:** \(u-u^*=0.8\) percentage points, so \(u = 4.8\%\).

**Explanation:** From \(-2=-2.5(u-u^*)\), cyclical unemployment is +0.8 percentage points. Add it to the natural rate to obtain actual unemployment.


**Common trap:** Keep percentage points and decimal fractions consistent.


*Tags: calculation, Okun*


### ch02-020 — Marginal product of labour · recall · D1

**Q:** What is the marginal product of labour (MPL)?

**Answer:** The additional output produced by one more unit of labour, holding other inputs such as capital fixed.

**Explanation:** The course assumes diminishing MPL: extra workers still raise output, but by progressively smaller amounts when capital and technology are fixed. MPL is central to the firm’s labour-demand decision.


**Common trap:** MPL is a change in output, not total output per worker.


*Tags: MPL, labour-demand*


### ch02-021 — Value of marginal product · formula · D1

**Q:** How is the value of the marginal product of labour calculated?

**Answer:** \(VMPL = p \times MPL\), where p is the firm’s output price.

**Explanation:** VMPL converts the physical extra output from a worker into extra revenue. A profit-maximising competitive firm hires labour while the additional revenue is at least the wage cost.


**Common trap:** Do not multiply MPL by the aggregate price level unless the firm’s output price is what is intended.


*Tags: formula, VMPL*


### ch02-022 — Firm labour demand · recall · D2

**Q:** What hiring rule determines a competitive firm’s labour demand?

**Answer:** Hire workers up to the point where the value of marginal product is approximately equal to the nominal wage: \(p\times MPL = W\).

**Explanation:** If the next worker’s VMPL exceeds W, hiring them raises profit; if VMPL is below W, they cost more than the extra revenue they generate. Diminishing MPL gives a downward-sloping labour-demand schedule.


**Common trap:** The rule is marginal, not “average product equals wage.”


*Tags: labour-demand, marginal-analysis*


### ch02-023 — Aggregate labour demand · formula · D2

**Q:** In aggregate real terms, what condition links labour demand to the real wage?

**Answer:** \(MPL\approx \frac{W}{P}\) at the competitive margin.

**Explanation:** When the firm’s output price moves with the aggregate price level, dividing \(p\times MPL = W\) by P yields a real-wage condition. Because MPL falls as employment rises, labour demand slopes downward in real-wage/employment space.


**Common trap:** The real wage is \(W/P\), not \(W\times P\).


*Tags: labour-demand, real-wage*


### ch02-024 — Labour demand shift · scenario · D2

**Q:** What happens to a firm’s labour-demand curve if the relative price of its output falls?

**Answer:** Labour demand shifts left/inward.

**Explanation:** A lower output price reduces VMPL at every employment level, making fewer workers profitable at any given wage. The equilibrium wage and employment at that firm tend to fall, other things equal.


**Common trap:** This is a shift of labour demand, not a movement along it caused by a wage change.


*Tags: shifts, labour-demand*


### ch02-025 — Labour demand shift · scenario · D1

**Q:** What happens to labour demand if worker productivity/MPL rises?

**Answer:** Labour demand shifts right/outward.

**Explanation:** Higher MPL raises the value produced by each worker and therefore raises VMPL. Firms are willing to employ more labour at any given real wage.


**Common trap:** A productivity change shifts demand; a real-wage change moves along demand.


*Tags: shifts, labour-demand*


### ch02-026 — Labour supply shift · scenario · D2

**Q:** Several nearby plants close, releasing workers with similar skills. What happens to labour supply to the remaining plant?

**Answer:** It shifts right/outward.

**Explanation:** More workers are available to the surviving employer at each wage. In the standard competitive diagram this tends to lower the equilibrium real wage and raise employment at the remaining plant.


**Common trap:** This is a change in local labour supply, not labour demand.


*Tags: shifts, labour-supply*


### ch02-027 — Wage floor · scenario · D1

**Q:** What does a binding wage floor or union wage above the competitive equilibrium do in the simple labour-market model?

**Answer:** It reduces employment demanded and creates excess labour supply/unemployment.

**Explanation:** At the higher real wage firms move up along their labour-demand curve and hire fewer workers, while more workers may wish to supply labour. The gap between supply and demand is unemployment in the model.


**Common trap:** A wage floor below equilibrium is non-binding and has no effect.


*Tags: wage-floor, unemployment*


### ch02-028 — Labour tax wedge · scenario · D3

**Q:** In the course model, what is the effect of an income tax on workers on labour supply and the wage wedge?

**Answer:** Labour supply shifts up/left; employment falls; the wage paid by firms exceeds the after-tax wage received by workers.

**Explanation:** For a given employer cost, workers keep less after tax, reducing willingness to work. The tax wedge is the difference between the real labour cost to firms and the real wage workers retain.


**Common trap:** Do not assume the entire tax burden necessarily falls on workers; equilibrium incidence depends on supply and demand.


*Tags: tax, labour-market*


### ch02-029 — Nominal vs real wage · scenario · D1

**Q:** If the price level and money wage both double, what happens to the real wage and labour demanded, other things equal?

**Answer:** The real wage \(W/P\) is unchanged, so labour demanded is unchanged.

**Explanation:** Doubling numerator and denominator leaves \(W/P\) constant. The chapter review uses this to test whether you distinguish nominal from real variables.


**Common trap:** A doubled money wage alone would raise the real wage if P did not also double.


*Tags: nominal-real, labour-demand*


### ch02-030 — Structural change · scenario · D2

**Q:** Self-service technology permanently displaces checkout workers whose skills do not match available jobs. Which unemployment component is most likely to rise persistently?

**Answer:** Structural unemployment.

**Explanation:** The key is persistent mismatch. There can be some short-lived frictional unemployment during normal search, but prolonged inability to match skills to jobs raises structural unemployment and therefore the natural rate.


**Common trap:** Technology-induced job loss is not automatically cyclical unemployment.


*Tags: unemployment-types, scenario*


### ch02-031 — Recession and unemployment · contrast · D2

**Q:** In the course’s basic definitions, how should a recession affect the natural and cyclical unemployment rates?

**Answer:** Cyclical unemployment rises; the natural rate is conceptually unchanged by the recession itself.

**Explanation:** The natural rate is defined as frictional plus structural unemployment. A recession creates a negative output gap and increases the cyclical component, although prolonged recessions could in reality have longer-run structural effects.


**Common trap:** For exam purposes use the model’s definition unless the question explicitly introduces hysteresis or structural change.


*Tags: recession, unemployment*


## Chapter 3: Interest Rates, Investment and Saving (32 cards)


### ch03-001 — Nominal interest · recall · D1

**Q:** What does a nominal interest rate measure?

**Answer:** The percentage increase in the number of dollars (or units of currency) promised over the period.

**Explanation:** A nominal return tells you how many more dollars you receive, but not how purchasing power changes. To get the real return, adjust for inflation over the same period.


**Common trap:** Do not subtract inflation when the question asks specifically for the nominal rate.


*Tags: interest-rates, nominal-real*


### ch03-002 — Gross vs net interest · formula · D1

**Q:** If the net nominal interest rate is i, what is the gross interest rate?

**Answer:** 1 + i.

**Explanation:** If $1 grows to $(1+i), the net rate is i and the gross growth factor is 1+i. Many multi-period formulas use gross rates because they compound multiplicatively.


**Common trap:** A 5% rate means \(i=0.05\) and gross rate 1.05, not 5.


*Tags: interest-rates, formula*


### ch03-003 — Exact real interest · formula · D2

**Q:** What is the exact relationship between nominal interest i, inflation \(\pi\) and the realized real interest rate r?

**Answer:** \(1+r=\frac{1+i}{1+\pi}\), so \(r=\frac{1+i}{1+\pi}-1\).

**Explanation:** The real rate compares how the nominal payoff changes relative to the price level. The familiar subtraction \(r\approx i-\pi\) is a close approximation for modest rates.


**Common trap:** The exact formula is multiplicative, not simply \(i-\pi\).


*Tags: interest-rates, formula*


### ch03-004 — Approximate real interest · formula · D1

**Q:** What is the common approximation for the real interest rate?

**Answer:** \(r\approx i-\pi\).

**Explanation:** For small percentage rates, the interaction term is tiny. Use actual inflation for an ex-post real rate and expected inflation for an ex-ante/expected real rate.


**Common trap:** Match actual versus expected inflation to the wording of the question.


*Tags: interest-rates, formula*


### ch03-005 — Ex-post vs expected real rate · contrast · D2

**Q:** What is the difference between ex-post and expected real interest rates?

**Answer:** Ex-post uses actual inflation after the fact; expected real interest uses expected inflation known when the decision is made.

**Explanation:** Investment and saving decisions generally depend on the expected real rate because future inflation is unknown at the time. Unexpected inflation creates a gap between expected and realized real returns.


**Common trap:** Do not use realized inflation to describe the rate a decision-maker expected beforehand.


*Tags: interest-rates, expectations*


### ch03-006 — Fisher effect · formula · D1

**Q:** State the Fisher effect used in the course.

**Answer:** \(i\approx r+\pi^e\); if the equilibrium real rate is fixed, expected inflation raises the nominal interest rate roughly one-for-one.

**Explanation:** The Fisher effect links nominal rates to expected inflation, not necessarily current realized inflation. With r fixed at 2%, expected inflation of 4% implies nominal \(i \approx 6\%\).


**Common trap:** It is expected inflation \(\pi^e\) that belongs in the ex-ante Fisher relation.


*Tags: Fisher-effect, formula*


### ch03-007 — Fisher effect · calculation · D1

**Q:** A deposit pays 1.75% nominal and a saver expects a 1.5% real return. What expected inflation is consistent with the approximate Fisher effect?

**Answer:** 0.25%.

**Explanation:** From \(i = r + \pi^e\), \(\pi^e = 1.75\% - 1.5\% = 0.25\%\). This is exactly the style of compact numerical question used in the chapter review.


**Common trap:** Subtract the real rate from the nominal rate, not vice versa.


*Tags: calculation, Fisher-effect*


### ch03-008 — Zero lower bound · scenario · D2

**Q:** If a nominal rate is stuck at zero and expected inflation becomes more negative, what happens to the expected real rate?

**Answer:** It rises.

**Explanation:** Using \(r\approx i-\pi^e\) with \(i=0\), \(r\approx-\pi^e\). More expected deflation therefore makes borrowing more expensive in real terms, which can weaken spending during a downturn.


**Common trap:** Deflation can tighten real monetary conditions even when nominal rates cannot be cut further.


*Tags: interest-rates, deflation*


### ch03-009 — Investment definition · recall · D1

**Q:** What does investment mean in macroeconomics?

**Answer:** Expenditure on newly produced capital goods plus inventory accumulation.

**Explanation:** It includes business fixed capital, residential construction and inventories. It is a real expenditure concept, distinct from acquiring an existing financial asset.


**Common trap:** Buying shares is financial saving, not macroeconomic investment I.


*Tags: investment, definition*


### ch03-010 — Capital stock · contrast · D1

**Q:** Why is investment a flow while capital is a stock?

**Answer:** Investment is measured over a period; the capital stock is measured at a point in time.

**Explanation:** Flows change stocks. Gross investment adds to the capital stock during a period while depreciation removes part of the existing stock.


**Common trap:** Never add a stock measured on a date directly to a flow measured per year without a stock-flow equation.


*Tags: stock-flow, investment*


### ch03-011 — Capital accumulation · formula · D1

**Q:** State the capital accumulation equation.

**Answer:** \(K_1 = K_0 + I - \delta K_0 = (1-\delta)K_0 + I\).

**Explanation:** The ending stock equals surviving old capital plus gross investment. Here \(\delta K_0\) is physical depreciation during the period.


**Common trap:** Some extracted course text contains a typo with a plus sign before depreciation; the lectures and economic logic use subtraction.


*Tags: capital, formula*


### ch03-012 — Net investment · formula · D1

**Q:** How is net investment defined?

**Answer:** Net investment = \(I-\delta K\).

**Explanation:** Positive net investment raises the capital stock; zero net investment keeps it constant; negative net investment means the capital stock shrinks.


**Common trap:** Gross investment can be positive while net investment is negative if depreciation is larger.


*Tags: investment, formula*


### ch03-013 — Capital accumulation · calculation · D2

**Q:** \(K_0=100\), \(I=20\) and \(K_1=115\). What depreciation rate \(\delta\) is implied?

**Answer:** 5%.

**Explanation:** \(115=100+20-\delta\times100\), so depreciation is 5 and \(\delta=0.05\). This calculation can also be reversed to find investment or the ending stock.


**Common trap:** Use depreciation on the beginning capital stock for this one-period equation.


*Tags: calculation, capital*


### ch03-014 — Marginal product of capital · recall · D1

**Q:** What is the marginal product of capital (MPK)?

**Answer:** The increase in output from one additional unit of capital, holding other inputs fixed.

**Explanation:** As with labour, the course typically assumes a positive but diminishing marginal product. This creates a ranking of investment projects from high to low marginal benefit.


**Common trap:** MPK is a physical output increment, not a dollar return.


*Tags: investment, MPK*


### ch03-015 — Value of marginal product of capital · formula · D1

**Q:** How do you calculate VMPK?

**Answer:** \(VMPK = p \times MPK\).

**Explanation:** Multiplying the extra physical output by its sale price gives the extra revenue benefit from another unit of capital. Firms compare this benefit with the user cost of capital.


**Common trap:** Compare VMPK to a dollar user cost, not to the interest rate alone unless units have already been normalised.


*Tags: investment, formula*


### ch03-016 — User cost of capital · recall · D2

**Q:** What is the user cost of capital intuitively?

**Answer:** The economic cost of owning and using a capital good for a period, including financing/opportunity cost, depreciation and any resale-price change.

**Explanation:** The purchase price alone is not the one-period cost because the firm still owns a depreciated asset at the end. The exact course formula subtracts the resale value of the depreciated capital good.


**Common trap:** Do not equate user cost with the sticker price of the capital good.


*Tags: investment, user-cost*


### ch03-017 — User cost of capital · formula · D3

**Q:** State the exact one-period user-cost formula from the course.

**Answer:** \(UC = P_K + iP_K - (1-\delta)(P_K + \Delta P_K)\).

**Explanation:** The firm pays the purchase price and financing/opportunity cost, then recovers the market value of the remaining depreciated asset at period end. This exact formula simplifies to a useful approximation.


**Common trap:** The final resale term is subtracted because it is value the firm retains.


*Tags: investment, formula, user-cost*


### ch03-018 — User cost approximation · formula · D2

**Q:** What is the approximate user cost if capital-goods prices rise at the general inflation rate?

**Answer:** \(UC\approx P_K(r+\delta)\), or in rate form \(uc\approx r+\delta\).

**Explanation:** Starting from \(UC\approx P_K[i+\delta-\Delta P_K/P_K]\), replacing capital-price inflation by \(\pi\) gives \(i-\pi=r\). Thus a higher real interest rate or depreciation rate raises the cost of holding capital.


**Common trap:** Use the real rate in the simplified formula, not the nominal rate plus inflation.


*Tags: investment, formula, user-cost*


### ch03-019 — Investment decision · recall · D2

**Q:** What marginal condition guides a firm’s investment decision?

**Answer:** Undertake an additional unit of investment if its expected VMPK is at least as large as its user cost.

**Explanation:** If marginal benefit exceeds marginal cost, the investment raises expected profit. Diminishing MPK means the firm stops once the next unit no longer covers its user cost.


**Common trap:** This is a marginal rule; sunk costs should not determine the next-unit decision.


*Tags: investment, marginal-analysis*


### ch03-020 — Investment demand · relationship · D1

**Q:** Why does desired investment slope downward with the real interest rate?

**Answer:** A higher real rate raises the user cost of capital, so fewer prospective investments have VMPK high enough to justify undertaking them.

**Explanation:** This gives I(r) a negative slope. A fall in r causes a movement along the investment schedule toward more investment.


**Common trap:** A change in expected profitability or productivity shifts the investment curve; r itself causes movement along it.


*Tags: investment, shifts*


### ch03-021 — Household saving · formula · D2

**Q:** What is household saving in the course national-accounts framework?

**Answer:** Household saving \(S_h=Y_D-C\).

**Explanation:** The textbook defines \(Y_D\) from household receipts net of taxes and retained earnings: \(Y_D=Y-TA+TR+INT-RE\) under its notation. Household saving is a flow that can add to net wealth.


**Common trap:** Do not confuse national income Y with household disposable income when taxes/transfers are present.


*Tags: saving, formula*


### ch03-022 — Wealth · formula · D1

**Q:** How is net wealth defined?

**Answer:** Net wealth = assets - liabilities.

**Explanation:** Wealth is a stock measured at a point in time. It can rise through saving or through capital gains on existing assets, so a change in wealth is not the same thing as saving.


**Common trap:** A rise in house prices can raise measured wealth without any current saving.


*Tags: wealth, stock-flow*


### ch03-023 — Wealth dynamics · formula · D2

**Q:** How does the course decompose the change in household wealth?

**Answer:** \(\Delta W=S+\text{net capital gains}\).

**Explanation:** Saving is the part arising from income not consumed; capital gains revalue existing assets. This explains why wealth-based and income-based measures of “saving” can differ.


**Common trap:** Do not count an asset-price gain as national-account saving.


*Tags: wealth, formula*


### ch03-024 — Life-cycle saving · recall · D2

**Q:** What is the basic life-cycle idea of household saving?

**Answer:** People use saving and borrowing to smooth consumption across periods in which income differs.

**Explanation:** A household may borrow or save little when young, save during high-earning years and run down assets in retirement. Current saving therefore depends on expected lifetime resources, not only current income.


**Common trap:** High current income does not imply the household wants current consumption to move one-for-one with income.


*Tags: saving, life-cycle*


### ch03-025 — Saving and real rates · relationship · D2

**Q:** In the course’s simple saving model, how does household saving respond to a higher real interest rate?

**Answer:** Household saving rises; the saving schedule slopes upward.

**Explanation:** A higher r raises the opportunity cost of current consumption relative to future consumption, generating a substitution toward saving. The course abstracts from ambiguous income effects and uses a positive aggregate relationship.


**Common trap:** For exam purposes follow the course’s upward-sloping saving schedule even though richer models can have ambiguous effects.


*Tags: saving, interest-rates*


### ch03-026 — Public saving · formula · D1

**Q:** How is public saving/budget balance defined in Chapter 3 notation?

**Answer:** Public saving = \(T-G\), where \(T\) is tax receipts net of transfers and government interest payments.

**Explanation:** A positive value is a budget surplus and adds to national saving; a negative value is a budget deficit and reduces national saving.


**Common trap:** Do not include transfers or interest payments in G; they enter net taxes T in this notation.


*Tags: saving, government, formula*


### ch03-027 — National saving · formula · D1

**Q:** How is national saving decomposed?

**Answer:** National saving = private saving + public saving = household saving + business saving + public saving.

**Explanation:** In a closed economy national saving is the pool of domestic resources available to finance investment. Retained earnings are business saving.


**Common trap:** National saving is not simply household saving.


*Tags: saving, formula*


### ch03-028 — Closed-economy equilibrium · recall · D1

**Q:** What condition determines the real interest rate in the closed-economy saving-investment model?

**Answer:** National saving equals investment: NS(r) = I(r).

**Explanation:** The real interest rate is the relative price that adjusts to clear the market for saving and investment. NS slopes upward and I slopes downward in the course model.


**Common trap:** Do not treat r as fixed in this Chapter 3 model; it is the endogenous equilibrating variable.


*Tags: saving-investment, equilibrium*


### ch03-029 — Crowding out · scenario · D2

**Q:** What happens in the closed-economy model when the government budget deficit increases, other things equal?

**Answer:** National saving shifts left, the equilibrium real interest rate rises, and private investment falls.

**Explanation:** The fall in public saving reduces the supply of national saving. The higher r restores \(NS=I\) by inducing more household saving and, crucially, less investment. The reduction in private investment is crowding out.


**Common trap:** The deficit does not directly shift the investment curve in this model.


*Tags: crowding-out, fiscal*


### ch03-030 — Investment slump · scenario · D2

**Q:** What happens if desired investment falls exogenously in the closed-economy saving-investment model?

**Answer:** The investment curve shifts left; the equilibrium real interest rate falls and equilibrium saving/investment falls.

**Explanation:** A weaker demand for investment funds lowers the relative price r. Because the saving schedule slopes upward, the lower r also reduces national saving until it equals the new lower investment level.


**Common trap:** Do not say saving is fixed: household saving responds to r in this model.


*Tags: investment, shifts*


### ch03-031 — Saving vs investment · scenario · D1

**Q:** You use a tax refund to buy existing Rio Tinto shares. Is that macroeconomic saving or investment?

**Answer:** Saving, not macroeconomic investment.

**Explanation:** You are acquiring an existing financial asset rather than financing the current production of a new capital good. The course repeatedly tests ordinary-language “investment” against the national-accounts definition.


**Common trap:** Financial investment is not I in GDP.


*Tags: classification, saving-investment*


### ch03-032 — Saving vs investment · scenario · D2

**Q:** A household buys a newly built house using a mortgage. How is this classified?

**Answer:** The new house is investment expenditure; taking on the mortgage is negative saving/dissaving for the household.

**Explanation:** Residential construction is included in macroeconomic investment even though households purchase it. Borrowing finances the acquisition by increasing liabilities.


**Common trap:** An existing-house sale would not itself be current investment.


*Tags: classification, saving-investment*


## Chapter 4: Income-Expenditure Model of GDP (29 cards)


### ch04-001 — Keynesian model assumption · recall · D1

**Q:** What key short-run assumption makes planned aggregate expenditure able to determine real GDP in the income-expenditure model?

**Answer:** Prices are fixed or sticky in the short run, so firms respond to unexpected demand by changing production rather than instantly changing prices.

**Explanation:** If prices fully adjusted to clear all markets immediately, expenditure shocks would not translate into the same short-run quantity adjustments. Sticky prices are therefore the bridge from changes in desired spending to changes in real output.


**Common trap:** Do not carry this fixed-price assumption into the later AD-AS model without qualification.


*Tags: Keynesian-model, assumption*


### ch04-002 — Actual vs planned expenditure · contrast · D2

**Q:** What is the difference between actual aggregate expenditure AE and planned aggregate expenditure PAE?

**Answer:** AE includes actual inventory investment, including unplanned inventory changes; PAE contains desired/planned spending, including planned inventory investment.

**Explanation:** Because the national accounts identity makes actual expenditure equal actual output, \(AE=Y\) by construction. PAE need not equal Y away from equilibrium, and the difference shows up as unplanned inventory accumulation or depletion.


**Common trap:** The equilibrium condition is \(PAE=Y\), not “\(AE=Y\),” because \(AE=Y\) is always true ex post.


*Tags: PAE, inventories*


### ch04-003 — PAE identity · formula · D1

**Q:** Write planned aggregate expenditure in the general open-economy form used in the course.

**Answer:** \(PAE = C + Iᴾ + G + X - M\).

**Explanation:** \(I^P\) is planned investment. In the two-sector model G, X and M are omitted; later chapters progressively add them back.


**Common trap:** Use planned investment \(I^P\) in behavioural equilibrium equations, not actual I when unplanned inventories may occur.


*Tags: PAE, formula*


### ch04-004 — Short-run equilibrium · formula · D1

**Q:** What is the short-run equilibrium condition in the income-expenditure model?

**Answer:** \(Y = PAE\).

**Explanation:** At equilibrium firms sell exactly the amount they planned to sell, so there is no unintended change in inventories prompting output adjustment. Graphically this is the intersection of the PAE curve with the 45-degree line.


**Common trap:** \(Y=AE\) is an accounting identity; \(Y=PAE\) is the behavioural equilibrium condition.


*Tags: equilibrium, PAE*


### ch04-005 — Disequilibrium inventories · scenario · D1

**Q:** If PAE < Y, what happens to inventories and what should firms do next?

**Answer:** There is an unplanned accumulation of inventories, so firms reduce production/output.

**Explanation:** Actual production exceeds desired purchases. Unsold goods pile up as unintended inventory investment, signalling firms to cut output until Y moves toward PAE.


**Common trap:** PAE<Y does not mean actual expenditure is less than GDP ex post; the inventory accumulation makes \(AE=Y\).


*Tags: inventories, disequilibrium*


### ch04-006 — Disequilibrium inventories · scenario · D1

**Q:** If PAE > Y, what happens to inventories and what should firms do next?

**Answer:** Inventories are unintentionally depleted, so firms increase production/output.

**Explanation:** Desired purchases exceed current production, so firms meet some demand by drawing down stocks. The unexpected inventory fall signals that production is too low.


**Common trap:** A decline in inventories can be positive evidence of excess demand rather than weak demand, depending on whether it was planned.


*Tags: inventories, disequilibrium*


### ch04-007 — Consumption function · formula · D1

**Q:** State the simple two-sector consumption function.

**Answer:** \(C = C_0 + cY\).

**Explanation:** \(C_0\) is autonomous consumption and c is the marginal propensity to consume (MPC), with 0<c<1. With no government sector, disposable income equals national income Y.


**Common trap:** Once taxes are introduced, consumption depends on disposable income \(Y-T\) rather than simply \(Y\).


*Tags: consumption, formula*


### ch04-008 — MPC · recall · D1

**Q:** What is the marginal propensity to consume (MPC)?

**Answer:** The increase in consumption caused by a one-unit increase in disposable income; in \(C=C_0+cY_D\), it is \(c\).

**Explanation:** If \(c=0.6\), an extra $100 of disposable income raises consumption by $60. The remainder is marginal saving in the simple model.


**Common trap:** MPC is a marginal change, not \(C/Y\).


*Tags: consumption, MPC*


### ch04-009 — APC · formula · D2

**Q:** How is the average propensity to consume (APC) defined?

**Answer:** \(APC = C / Y^D\).

**Explanation:** Unlike the MPC, APC generally changes with income when autonomous consumption \(C_0\) is nonzero. For \(C=C_0+cY^D\), \(APC=C_0/Y^D+c\) and tends toward c as income grows.


**Common trap:** Do not confuse APC with the slope of the consumption function.


*Tags: consumption, APC*


### ch04-010 — Autonomous vs induced expenditure · contrast · D2

**Q:** What is autonomous expenditure versus induced expenditure?

**Answer:** Autonomous expenditure does not depend on current Y; induced expenditure changes with Y.

**Explanation:** In a simple PAE curve, the intercept collects autonomous components such as \(C_0\) and \(I^P\), while cY is induced consumption. In open/government models taxes and imports change the induced slope as well.


**Common trap:** “Exogenous” and “autonomous” are closely related here, but an exogenous variable can enter a behavioural equation in more than one way.


*Tags: PAE, terminology*


### ch04-011 — Two-sector equilibrium · formula · D2

**Q:** For \(C=C_0+cY\) and planned investment \(I^P=I_0\), what is equilibrium GDP?

**Answer:** \(Y^e=\frac{C_0+I_0}{1-c}\).

**Explanation:** Set \(Y=C_0+cY+I_0\) and collect \(Y\) terms: \((1-c)Y=C_0+I_0\). The denominator is the leakage into saving from each extra dollar of income.


**Common trap:** Do not forget to divide the entire autonomous-expenditure sum by 1-c.


*Tags: equilibrium, formula*


### ch04-012 — Multiplier · formula · D1

**Q:** What is the two-sector income-expenditure multiplier?

**Answer:** \(k = 1/(1-c)\).

**Explanation:** A one-unit rise in autonomous spending first raises output by one, then induces c more consumption, then \(c^{2}\), and so on. The geometric series sums to 1/(1-c).


**Common trap:** The multiplier is larger when MPC c is larger, not smaller.


*Tags: multiplier, formula*


### ch04-013 — Multiplier · calculation · D1

**Q:** If \(c=0.6\) and autonomous planned investment rises by 20, what is ΔY?

**Answer:** Multiplier = \(\frac{1}{1-0.6}=2.5\), so \(\Delta Y=50\).

**Explanation:** The first round is +20 output, the next induced-consumption round is +12, then +7.2, etc. Summing all rounds gives \(20\times2.5=50\).


**Common trap:** Do not stop after the first-round spending change.


*Tags: calculation, multiplier*


### ch04-014 — Multiplier process · recall · D2

**Q:** Why does an autonomous expenditure change have a multiplied effect on equilibrium GDP?

**Answer:** One person’s spending becomes another person’s income, part of which is spent again, creating successive induced-expenditure rounds.

**Explanation:** Each round is smaller because only the MPC fraction is respent. Leakages such as saving, taxes and imports determine how quickly the process dies out.


**Common trap:** The multiplier does not create unlimited output; with 0<c<1 the geometric series converges.


*Tags: multiplier, intuition*


### ch04-015 — 45-degree diagram · recall · D1

**Q:** What does the 45-degree line represent in the income-expenditure diagram?

**Answer:** All points where planned/actual expenditure on the vertical axis equals output Y on the horizontal axis.

**Explanation:** Its slope is one because the same unit is measured on both axes. The equilibrium is where the PAE curve intersects this line.


**Common trap:** The 45-degree line is not the PAE curve itself.


*Tags: graph, PAE*


### ch04-016 — PAE shift · scenario · D1

**Q:** What happens to the PAE curve and equilibrium Y if planned investment falls exogenously?

**Answer:** PAE shifts downward by the investment fall; equilibrium Y falls by the change times the multiplier.

**Explanation:** Investment is autonomous in the basic model, so its change alters the PAE intercept rather than the slope. Inventory disequilibrium starts the adjustment process.


**Common trap:** Do not move along a fixed PAE curve when an autonomous component changes.


*Tags: shifts, PAE*


### ch04-017 — Saving function · formula · D2

**Q:** Derive the two-sector saving function from \(C=C_0+cY\).

**Answer:** \(S = Y - C = -C_0 + (1-c)Y\).

**Explanation:** Autonomous consumption implies negative saving at sufficiently low income. The marginal propensity to save is 1-c in this simple no-government model.


**Common trap:** The intercept of saving is \(-C_0\), not \(+C_0\).


*Tags: saving, formula*


### ch04-018 — Saving-investment equilibrium · formula · D2

**Q:** What is the equivalent equilibrium condition in the two-sector model expressed using saving?

**Answer:** \(S = Iᴾ\).

**Explanation:** Starting from \(Y=C+Iᴾ\) and subtracting C from both sides gives \(Y-C=Iᴾ\), and Y-C is saving. This is the same equilibrium as \(PAE=Y\), expressed as leakages equal injections.


**Common trap:** This Chapter 4 equality determines Y given planned investment; Chapter 3 uses \(NS=I\) to determine r. Same accounting relation, different model closure.


*Tags: equilibrium, saving-investment*


### ch04-019 — Paradox of thrift · recall · D2

**Q:** What is the paradox of thrift in the basic Keynesian model?

**Answer:** If everyone tries to save more by cutting autonomous consumption, equilibrium income falls, and aggregate saving may fail to rise.

**Explanation:** A fall in \(C_0\) shifts PAE down and reduces Y through the multiplier. In the two-sector equilibrium, actual saving must still equal fixed planned investment, so the collective attempt to save more does not raise aggregate equilibrium saving.


**Common trap:** Individual prudence need not aggregate to higher total saving when income is endogenous.


*Tags: paradox-of-thrift, saving*


### ch04-020 — Open economy imports · formula · D1

**Q:** How are imports modelled in the simple open-economy income-expenditure model?

**Answer:** \(M = mY\), where m is the marginal propensity to import.

**Explanation:** As domestic income rises, residents buy more imports, creating a leakage from spending on domestic production. This reduces the slope of PAE relative to a closed economy.


**Common trap:** Imports are subtracted in PAE because they are expenditure on foreign output.


*Tags: open-economy, imports*


### ch04-021 — Open-economy PAE · formula · D2

**Q:** With \(C=C_0+cY\), \(Iᴾ=I_0\), \(X=X_0\) and \(M=mY\), what is PAE?

**Answer:** \(PAE = C_0 + I_0 + X_0 + (c-m)Y\).

**Explanation:** Exports and planned investment are autonomous injections; induced consumption raises PAE with Y while induced imports reduce spending on domestic output.


**Common trap:** The slope is c-m, not c+m.


*Tags: open-economy, PAE*


### ch04-022 — Open-economy multiplier · formula · D2

**Q:** What is the multiplier in the simple open economy with no government?

**Answer:** \(k = 1/(1-c+m)\).

**Explanation:** Imports add another leakage. Compared with the two-sector multiplier 1/(1-c), a positive m makes the denominator larger and the multiplier smaller.


**Common trap:** The import term enters the denominator with a plus sign because M is subtracted from expenditure.


*Tags: open-economy, multiplier*


### ch04-023 — Open-economy equilibrium · formula · D2

**Q:** What leakage-injection condition is equivalent to equilibrium in the open economy with no government?

**Answer:** \(S+M=I^P+X\).

**Explanation:** Saving and imports are leakages from the domestic expenditure stream; planned investment and exports are injections. Equality is algebraically equivalent to \(Y=PAE\).


**Common trap:** Net exports alone are not the full injection side; use X explicitly in this leakage-injection form.


*Tags: open-economy, equilibrium*


### ch04-024 — Open economy · calculation · D2

**Q:** \(C=200+0.6Y\), \(Iᴾ=80\), \(X=60\) and \(M=0.1Y\). Find equilibrium Y.

**Answer:** \(PAE=340+0.5Y\), so \(Y=680\).

**Explanation:** Set \(Y=200+0.6Y+80+60-0.1Y\). Then \(0.5Y=340\). The multiplier is \(\frac{1}{1-0.6+0.1}=2\).


**Common trap:** Combine induced terms carefully: \(0.6Y-0.1Y=0.5Y\).


*Tags: calculation, open-economy*


### ch04-025 — Output adjustment · scenario · D2

**Q:** At \(Y=800\), \(PAE=790\). What is the unplanned inventory change and expected direction of Y?

**Answer:** Unplanned inventory accumulation is 10; firms are expected to reduce Y.

**Explanation:** Because production exceeds planned spending by 10, the unsold output becomes unintended inventories. Firms respond by cutting production until \(PAE=Y\).


**Common trap:** The sign of unplanned inventory investment is \(Y-PAE\) here, not \(PAE-Y\).


*Tags: calculation, inventories*


### ch04-026 — Parameters and variables · recall · D2

**Q:** In \(C=100+0.8Y\) and \(Iᴾ=50\), identify a parameter, an exogenous variable and an endogenous variable.

**Answer:** 0.8 (and 100) are parameters; \(I^P=50\) is exogenous; Y and C are endogenous in the model.

**Explanation:** Parameters specify behavioural relationships; exogenous variables are taken as given; endogenous variables are solved within the model. The course often asks this classification before algebra.


**Common trap:** A constant can be a parameter or an exogenous variable depending on what it represents; interpret the model, not just the number.


*Tags: models, terminology*


### ch04-027 — Multiplier size · relationship · D1

**Q:** What happens to the multiplier when the MPC rises, all else equal?

**Answer:** It rises.

**Explanation:** With a larger c, less of each extra dollar leaks into saving and more is respent, so the chain of induced expenditure decays more slowly. Algebraically 1/(1-c) increases as c increases.


**Common trap:** A high MPC makes the economy more responsive to autonomous spending shocks.


*Tags: multiplier, comparative-statics*


### ch04-028 — Multiplier size · relationship · D1

**Q:** What happens to the open-economy multiplier when the marginal propensity to import m rises?

**Answer:** It falls.

**Explanation:** More of each extra dollar of income leaks into imported goods rather than demand for domestic output. In \(k=1/(1-c+m)\), a larger m increases the denominator.


**Common trap:** Imports are a leakage, so greater import responsiveness dampens domestic multiplier effects.


*Tags: multiplier, open-economy*


### ch04-029 — PAE vs GDP identity · exam-trap · D3

**Q:** Why can actual aggregate expenditure equal GDP even when the economy is not in income-expenditure equilibrium?

**Answer:** Because unplanned inventory investment adjusts ex post so that actual expenditure equals the value of output; equilibrium additionally requires planned expenditure to equal output.

**Explanation:** This distinction is one of the most testable conceptual traps in Chapter 4. Disequilibrium is revealed by unintended inventories, not by a violation of the national-accounts identity.


**Common trap:** Never say AE≠Y as the definition of disequilibrium in this model.


*Tags: exam-trap, PAE*


## Chapter 5: Government Sector and Fiscal Policy (33 cards)


### ch05-001 — Fiscal policy · recall · D1

**Q:** What is fiscal policy in this course?

**Answer:** Government decisions about purchases/spending, taxes and transfers that affect aggregate economic activity and the budget.

**Explanation:** In the income-expenditure model, G affects planned expenditure directly while taxes and transfers affect it indirectly through disposable income and consumption. Fiscal policy can be discretionary or operate automatically through existing rules.


**Common trap:** Do not confuse fiscal policy with RBA interest-rate policy, which is monetary policy.


*Tags: fiscal-policy, definition*


### ch05-002 — Tax function · formula · D1

**Q:** State the tax function used in the three-sector model.

**Answer:** \(T = T_0 + tY\).

**Explanation:** \(T_0\) is autonomous net tax revenue and t is the marginal tax rate. Because some taxes rise and some transfers fall automatically as income rises, net taxes are positively related to GDP.


**Common trap:** \(t\) is a marginal rate, not the average tax rate \(T/Y\).


*Tags: tax, formula*


### ch05-003 — Marginal vs average tax rate · contrast · D2

**Q:** How do the marginal and average tax rates differ in \(T=T_0+tY\)?

**Answer:** Marginal tax rate = t; average tax rate = \(T/Y = T_0/Y + t\).

**Explanation:** The marginal rate tells you how much extra tax is generated by one more unit of income. The average rate depends on the level of Y whenever \(T_0\) is nonzero.


**Common trap:** Do not read \(T_0\) as a tax rate; it is an autonomous dollar amount.


*Tags: tax, terminology*


### ch05-004 — Consumption with taxes · formula · D2

**Q:** Substitute \(T=T_0+tY\) into \(C=C_0+c(Y-T)\). What consumption function results?

**Answer:** \(C = C_0 - cT_0 + c(1-t)Y\).

**Explanation:** Only after-tax income induces consumption. The effective MPC out of national income is c(1-t), smaller than the MPC c out of disposable income.


**Common trap:** The course review has a likely typo in one bracketed answer; algebraically the MPC out of disposable income is c, while out of Y it is c(1-t).


*Tags: consumption, tax, formula*


### ch05-005 — Three-sector PAE · formula · D2

**Q:** What is PAE in the closed three-sector model with taxes?

**Answer:** \(PAE = [C_0 - cT_0 + I_0 + G_0] + c(1-t)Y\).

**Explanation:** The bracketed term is autonomous expenditure. The slope is c(1-t), reflecting the fact that taxes siphon off part of each extra unit of income before it can generate consumption.


**Common trap:** Do not add T directly to PAE; taxes influence C through disposable income.


*Tags: PAE, fiscal, formula*


### ch05-006 — Three-sector equilibrium · formula · D2

**Q:** What is equilibrium GDP in the closed three-sector model?

**Answer:** \(Y^e=\frac{C_0-cT_0+I_0+G_0}{1-c(1-t)}\).

**Explanation:** Set \(Y=PAE\) and collect induced expenditure on the left. Compared with the two-sector model, the proportional tax rate makes the multiplier smaller.


**Common trap:** Keep the whole autonomous bracket in the numerator.


*Tags: equilibrium, fiscal, formula*


### ch05-007 — Government spending multiplier · formula · D1

**Q:** What is the government expenditure multiplier in the closed three-sector model?

**Answer:** \(k_G = 1 / [1 - c(1-t)]\).

**Explanation:** An extra dollar of G enters PAE dollar-for-dollar in the first round, then creates induced consumption in later rounds. The automatic tax response reduces those subsequent rounds.


**Common trap:** \(k_G\) is not always the simple 1/(1-c) once t>0.


*Tags: fiscal-multiplier, formula*


### ch05-008 — Autonomous tax multiplier · formula · D2

**Q:** What is the autonomous tax multiplier?

**Answer:** \(k_T = -c / [1 - c(1-t)]\).

**Explanation:** A $1 increase in autonomous taxes reduces disposable income by $1 but cuts first-round consumption only by c dollars, so its impact is smaller in absolute value than the G multiplier. A tax cut therefore raises Y.


**Common trap:** The sign is negative for a tax increase. A transfer increase has the opposite sign because it raises disposable income.


*Tags: fiscal-multiplier, formula*


### ch05-009 — Fiscal multiplier comparison · recall · D1

**Q:** Why is |\(k_T\)| smaller than \(k_G\)?

**Answer:** Because a change in G affects PAE one-for-one initially, whereas a tax change affects PAE only through the fraction c of disposable-income change that households consume.

**Explanation:** Algebraically |\(k_T\)| = c \(k_G\) and 0<c<1. This is why equal-sized spending and tax changes need not offset each other.


**Common trap:** Do not assume a $100 tax cut and $100 G increase have identical GDP effects.


*Tags: fiscal-multiplier, intuition*


### ch05-010 — Balanced-budget multiplier · formula · D3

**Q:** What is the balanced-budget multiplier when \(\Delta G_0=\Delta T_0\) in this model?

**Answer:** \(k_{BB}=\frac{1-c}{1-c(1-t)}=k_G+k_T\).

**Explanation:** The spending increase raises PAE by the full \(\Delta G\), while the equal tax increase initially reduces consumption only by \(c\Delta T\). The net first-round injection is \((1-c)\Delta G\), which is then multiplied.


**Common trap:** It equals 1 only in the special case \(t=0\); with proportional taxes it can be less than 1.


*Tags: fiscal-multiplier, balanced-budget*


### ch05-011 — Balanced-budget multiplier · calculation · D2

**Q:** If \(c=0.8\) and \(t=0.3\), what is \(k_BB\)?

**Answer:** \(\frac{1-0.8}{1-0.8(0.7)}=\frac{0.2}{0.44}\approx0.45\).

**Explanation:** The textbook uses this example to show that the claim “balanced-budget multiplier equals one” is not generally true once proportional taxes are present.


**Common trap:** Do not use \(k_G-k_T\) with \(k_T\) treated as a positive magnitude; \(k_T\) already carries a negative sign.


*Tags: calculation, balanced-budget*


### ch05-012 — Automatic stabilisers · recall · D1

**Q:** What is an automatic fiscal stabiliser?

**Answer:** A tax or transfer rule that changes with economic activity and dampens fluctuations without a new discretionary policy decision.

**Explanation:** When Y falls, tax receipts fall and some transfers rise, supporting disposable income; when Y rises, the reverse occurs. In the model, a positive t lowers the multiplier and therefore dampens autonomous shocks.


**Common trap:** Automatic does not mean instantaneous perfection; it means no new legislation or one-off decision is required for the response.


*Tags: automatic-stabiliser, fiscal*


### ch05-013 — Automatic stabilisers · relationship · D2

**Q:** Why does a higher marginal tax rate t reduce the income-expenditure multiplier?

**Answer:** It makes a larger fraction of each extra dollar of income leak into net taxes, so less becomes induced consumption.

**Explanation:** In \(k=1/[1-c(1-t)]\), a larger t reduces c(1-t), increases the denominator and lowers k. This is the algebraic stabilisation mechanism.


**Common trap:** The stabilising effect concerns the response to shocks, not necessarily the desirability of any tax rate.


*Tags: automatic-stabiliser, multiplier*


### ch05-014 — Discretionary fiscal policy · recall · D1

**Q:** What is discretionary fiscal policy?

**Answer:** A deliberate change in fiscal instruments—such as G, autonomous taxes or transfers—in response to actual or expected economic conditions.

**Explanation:** Unlike automatic stabilisers, discretionary actions require diagnosis, design, political approval and implementation. These lags can make timing difficult.


**Common trap:** Do not call the normal cyclical fall in tax receipts a discretionary tax cut.


*Tags: discretionary-policy, fiscal*


### ch05-015 — Fiscal policy lags · recall · D2

**Q:** Why can discretionary fiscal stabilisation be poorly timed?

**Answer:** Recognition, decision/legislative, implementation and economic-effect lags can mean the policy arrives after conditions have changed.

**Explanation:** The Chapter 8 tutorial revisits this with a delayed tax cut that can become expansionary after the recession has already ended. Policy timing is therefore part of the exam-style reasoning, not just multiplier arithmetic.


**Common trap:** A policy can be directionally sensible when announced yet destabilising when it actually hits the economy.


*Tags: fiscal-policy, lags*


### ch05-016 — Fiscal policy and output gap · scenario · D2

**Q:** How can fiscal policy close a contractionary output gap in the income-expenditure model?

**Answer:** Shift PAE upward using higher G, lower autonomous taxes, or higher transfers by enough that new equilibrium Y equals Y*.

**Explanation:** The required instrument change equals the desired \(\Delta Y\) divided by the relevant fiscal multiplier. The choice of instrument matters because spending and tax multipliers differ.


**Common trap:** Use the gap in levels for multiplier arithmetic unless the question explicitly works in percentages.


*Tags: output-gap, fiscal-policy*


### ch05-017 — Fiscal policy and output gap · scenario · D1

**Q:** How should contractionary fiscal policy affect an expansionary output gap?

**Answer:** Lower G and/or raise taxes/reduce transfers to shift PAE down and reduce equilibrium Y toward Y*.

**Explanation:** An expansionary gap means demand/output is above sustainable potential. Contractionary fiscal policy reduces autonomous demand.


**Common trap:** “Expansionary gap” describes the state of the economy, not the policy response you should use.


*Tags: output-gap, fiscal-policy*


### ch05-018 — Budget balance · formula · D1

**Q:** How is the government budget balance BB defined in the textbook notation?

**Answer:** \(BB = T - G\), where T is tax revenue net of transfers and government interest payments.

**Explanation:** \(BB>0\) is a surplus; \(BB<0\) is a deficit. In expanded notation, \(BB=\text{tax receipts}-\text{transfers}-\text{interest}-\text{government purchases}\).


**Common trap:** Government interest payments reduce the budget balance even though they are not part of G in GDP.


*Tags: budget, formula*


### ch05-019 — Primary budget balance · recall · D2

**Q:** What is the primary budget balance?

**Answer:** The budget balance excluding interest payments on existing government debt.

**Explanation:** Using tax receipts \(\tilde T\), transfers TR and purchases G, \(PBB = \tilde T - G - TR\). It isolates the current fiscal stance from the inherited cost of servicing past debt.


**Common trap:** A government can have a primary surplus but an overall deficit if interest payments are large.


*Tags: budget, public-debt*


### ch05-020 — Debt stock and deficit flow · contrast · D1

**Q:** Why are the budget deficit and public debt different concepts?

**Answer:** The deficit/surplus is a flow over a period; public debt is a stock at a point in time.

**Explanation:** A deficit adds to the debt stock, while a surplus reduces it. This stock-flow distinction is repeatedly tested throughout the course.


**Common trap:** A large debt does not imply the current-year budget is necessarily in deficit.


*Tags: stock-flow, public-debt*


### ch05-021 — Debt dynamics · formula · D1

**Q:** What is the simple government debt accumulation identity?

**Answer:** \(D_t = D_{t-1} - BB_t\).

**Explanation:** Because a deficit has BB<0, subtracting it increases debt. A surplus has BB>0 and therefore reduces debt.


**Common trap:** The sign convention makes deficits negative budget balances.


*Tags: public-debt, formula*


### ch05-022 — Government budget constraint · formula · D3

**Q:** State the expanded government budget constraint.

**Answer:** \(G_t + TR_t + rD_{t-1} = \tilde{T}_t + (D_t-D_{t-1})\).

**Explanation:** Current purchases, transfers and real interest payments must be financed by tax revenue or new borrowing. It is an accounting constraint on government funding, not a claim that every government should balance its budget each period.


**Common trap:** Borrowing is a financing source on the right-hand side, not revenue from production.


*Tags: public-debt, formula*


### ch05-023 — Debt financing · recall · D2

**Q:** How does a government normally finance a budget deficit in this framework?

**Answer:** By issuing new government bonds/borrowing, which increases the stock of public debt.

**Explanation:** The government can borrow from domestic or foreign private sectors; direct central-bank financing is treated separately and historically has greater inflation risk when used persistently at large scale.


**Common trap:** Deficit spending and “printing money” are not synonymous in the standard setup.


*Tags: public-debt, financing*


### ch05-024 — Debt-to-GDP ratio · recall · D1

**Q:** Why is debt-to-GDP often more informative than the dollar level of debt?

**Answer:** It scales the debt burden by the economy’s capacity to generate income/output.

**Explanation:** The same dollar debt is easier to service in a much larger economy. Sustainability analysis therefore focuses on \(d=D/Y\) rather than D alone.


**Common trap:** A rising nominal debt can coexist with a stable or falling debt-to-GDP ratio if GDP grows sufficiently fast.


*Tags: public-debt, ratio*


### ch05-025 — Debt sustainability · formula · D3

**Q:** What approximate relation drives changes in the debt-to-GDP ratio in the textbook?

**Answer:** \(\Delta d = \frac{r-g}{1+g}d_{t-1} - pbb_t\).

**Explanation:** The existing debt ratio tends to rise if the real interest rate exceeds real GDP growth, unless offset by a primary surplus. Faster growth and lower interest rates make stabilisation easier.


**Common trap:** \(pbb\) is the primary balance as a share of GDP; a primary surplus is positive and lowers \(\Delta d\).


*Tags: public-debt, sustainability, formula*


### ch05-026 — Debt stabilisation · scenario · D2

**Q:** If r>g and the government runs a zero primary balance, what happens to the debt-to-GDP ratio?

**Answer:** It rises.

**Explanation:** Interest compounds the existing debt faster than the denominator GDP grows. To hold the ratio constant when r>g, the government generally needs a primary surplus.


**Common trap:** Zero overall deficit and zero primary deficit are different once interest payments exist.


*Tags: public-debt, sustainability*


### ch05-027 — Debt stabilisation · scenario · D3

**Q:** If g>r, how does that affect the primary balance required to stabilise debt/GDP?

**Answer:** It makes stabilisation easier; the government may be able to run some primary deficit while keeping the ratio stable.

**Explanation:** When growth outpaces the real interest rate, the existing debt stock shrinks relative to GDP mechanically. The exact allowable primary deficit depends on the starting debt ratio and the r-g gap.


**Common trap:** This does not mean deficits are costless or unlimited.


*Tags: public-debt, sustainability*


### ch05-028 — Public debt costs · recall · D2

**Q:** Name the three broad concerns about high public debt emphasised in the textbook.

**Answer:** Potential effects on economic growth, crowding out of private investment through higher interest rates, and intergenerational equity.

**Explanation:** The text also stresses that empirical growth thresholds are contested. The conceptual mechanisms matter more for an introductory MCQ than memorising a particular disputed threshold.


**Common trap:** Do not treat a specific debt/GDP threshold as an iron economic law.


*Tags: public-debt, costs*


### ch05-029 — Golden rule · recall · D2

**Q:** What is the fiscal “golden rule” discussed in the textbook?

**Answer:** Finance current government consumption from current taxes, but borrowing can be justified for productive long-lived public investment that benefits future generations.

**Explanation:** The intergenerational logic is “pay as you use”: future taxpayers who receive some of the infrastructure’s benefits also bear some of its financing cost.


**Common trap:** The rule does not say all government borrowing is good; it links borrowing to durable investment benefits.


*Tags: public-debt, fiscal-rule*


### ch05-030 — Balanced over the cycle · recall · D2

**Q:** What does “balance the budget over the business cycle” mean?

**Answer:** Allow deficits in recessions and seek surpluses in expansions so that cyclical deficits are roughly offset over time.

**Explanation:** Forcing immediate balance during a recession could require tax rises or spending cuts that deepen a contractionary gap. The rule aims to preserve stabilisation while avoiding persistently rising debt.


**Common trap:** It does not require \(BB=0\) in every single year.


*Tags: fiscal-rule, business-cycle*


### ch05-031 — Four-sector model · formula · D2

**Q:** What is PAE in the four-sector model with proportional taxes and imports?

**Answer:** \(PAE = [C_0 - cT_0 + G_0 + I_0 + X_0] + [c(1-t)-m]Y\).

**Explanation:** Taxes and imports both reduce induced spending on domestic output. The intercept contains autonomous consumption net of autonomous-tax effects plus autonomous investment, government purchases and exports.


**Common trap:** The slope is c(1-t)-m; imports are subtracted.


*Tags: four-sector, PAE*


### ch05-032 — Four-sector multiplier · formula · D2

**Q:** What is the four-sector multiplier?

**Answer:** \(k=\frac{1}{1-c(1-t)+m}\).

**Explanation:** The denominator contains leakages into saving, net taxes and imports. Higher t or m lowers the multiplier; higher c raises it.


**Common trap:** The plus sign before m in the denominator follows from imports being a leakage.


*Tags: four-sector, multiplier*


### ch05-033 — Leakages and injections · formula · D2

**Q:** What is the four-sector equilibrium condition in leakage-injection form?

**Answer:** S + T + \(M = Iᴾ + G + X\).

**Explanation:** Private saving, net taxes and imports are leakages from the circular flow; planned investment, government purchases and exports are injections. This is algebraically equivalent to \(Y=PAE\).


**Common trap:** Be consistent about whether T means net taxes in the course notation.


*Tags: four-sector, equilibrium*


## Chapter 6: Financial Assets, Money and Private Banks (31 cards)


### ch06-001 — Asset return · formula · D2

**Q:** How do you calculate the one-period gross and net rate of return on an asset?

**Answer:** Gross return = \(\frac{P_1+\text{income}}{P_0}\); net rate of return = \(\frac{P_1+\text{income}-P_0}{P_0}\).

**Explanation:** An asset rewards its holder through any cash income plus capital gain or loss. A gross return of 1.08 corresponds to an 8% net return.


**Common trap:** Do not divide the capital gain alone by the final price; returns are measured relative to the initial purchase price.


*Tags: assets, return, formula*


### ch06-002 — Bond terminology · recall · D1

**Q:** What are a bond’s principal (face value), coupon and maturity?

**Answer:** Principal/face value is the promised amount repaid at maturity; the coupon is any periodic interest payment; maturity is the date/term until final repayment.

**Explanation:** A zero-coupon bond has no periodic coupon and instead pays its face value at maturity. Bond prices today depend on the present value of promised future payments.


**Common trap:** The market price of a bond need not equal its face value.


*Tags: bonds, definition*


### ch06-003 — Bond price · formula · D1

**Q:** What is the price today of a one-period zero-coupon bond that pays F next period when the market interest rate is i?

**Answer:** \(P = F/(1+i)\).

**Explanation:** The bond price is the present value of its future payment. A higher required interest rate discounts the same future payment more heavily.


**Common trap:** Do not multiply F by (1+i); that would compound a present amount forward rather than discount a future payment back.


*Tags: bonds, present-value, formula*


### ch06-004 — Bond price and interest rate · relationship · D1

**Q:** What happens to an existing bond’s market price when market interest rates rise?

**Answer:** Its price falls.

**Explanation:** The bond’s promised payments are fixed. New securities now offer a higher return, so the old bond must become cheaper to offer the same market yield. Thus bond prices and yields move inversely.


**Common trap:** This is about the market price of an already-issued bond, not necessarily the face value repaid at maturity.


*Tags: bonds, interest-rate, high-yield*


### ch06-005 — Bond price · calculation · D1

**Q:** A one-period bond pays $100 next year. What is its price if \(i=4\%\)?

**Answer:** \(100/1.04\approx\$96.15\).

**Explanation:** This is the exact worksheet calculation. At 2.5%, the same bond would be worth about $97.56, illustrating the inverse price-interest-rate relation.


**Common trap:** Use the market rate in decimal form: 0.04, not 4.


*Tags: bonds, calculation*


### ch06-006 — Money functions · recall · D1

**Q:** What three functions of money are emphasised in the course?

**Answer:** Medium of exchange, unit of account and store of value.

**Explanation:** As a medium of exchange money avoids barter’s double coincidence of wants; as a unit of account it provides a common price measure; as a store of value it transfers purchasing power through time.


**Common trap:** Money can be an imperfect store of value during inflation and still count as money.


*Tags: money, definition*


### ch06-007 — Liquidity · recall · D1

**Q:** What does it mean for an asset to be liquid?

**Answer:** It can be converted quickly and cheaply into the medium of exchange with little loss of value.

**Explanation:** Currency and transaction deposits are highly liquid. Houses and specialised machinery are much less liquid even though they can be valuable stores of wealth.


**Common trap:** Liquidity is not the same as profitability or solvency.


*Tags: money, liquidity*


### ch06-008 — Fiat money · recall · D1

**Q:** What is fiat money?

**Answer:** Money whose value is not based on conversion into a commodity but on its accepted monetary status and confidence that others will accept it.

**Explanation:** Modern currency is valuable because it is widely accepted for payments and supported by monetary/legal institutions, not because the notes themselves have equivalent intrinsic commodity value.


**Common trap:** Do not confuse fiat money with commodity money such as gold used directly as money.


*Tags: money, definition*


### ch06-009 — Money stock · formula · D2

**Q:** In the simplified banking model, what makes up the money stock?

**Answer:** Money stock is \(M=C_u+D\): currency held by the public plus bank deposits usable for payment..

**Explanation:** Deposits count because they can directly make payments. Currency sitting inside a bank’s vault or settlement account is not simultaneously currency held by the public.


**Common trap:** Do not add bank reserves to public currency and deposits as an extra component of ordinary money; that would double-count monetary-base assets as if they were public deposits.


*Tags: money-supply, formula*


### ch06-010 — Money demand · recall · D2

**Q:** Why do households and firms demand money balances?

**Answer:** Primarily to conduct transactions and as a liquid asset, trading convenience against the interest return forgone on other assets.

**Explanation:** Higher income/output generates more transactions and raises desired money balances. A higher nominal interest rate raises the opportunity cost of holding non-interest-bearing money and lowers desired balances.


**Common trap:** The relevant opportunity cost is usually the nominal interest rate because money and bonds are nominal financial assets.


*Tags: money-demand, intuition*


### ch06-011 — Money demand · relationship · D1

**Q:** How does higher real income \(Y\) affect the real money-demand curve \(MD/P\)?

**Answer:** It shifts real money demand to the right.

**Explanation:** At any given nominal interest rate, a higher volume of transactions makes people want to hold more purchasing power as money.


**Common trap:** Do not describe this as a movement along MD: Y is a shift variable, not an axis variable when the diagram plots money balances against i.


*Tags: money-demand, shift-vs-movement*


### ch06-012 — Money demand · relationship · D1

**Q:** How does a rise in the nominal interest rate i affect money demanded, holding Y and P fixed?

**Answer:** It causes a movement along the money-demand curve toward a lower quantity of money demanded.

**Explanation:** The higher return available on alternative interest-bearing assets raises the opportunity cost of holding money.


**Common trap:** Because i is normally on the vertical axis, a change in i is a movement along the curve rather than a shift of it.


*Tags: money-demand, shift-vs-movement*


### ch06-013 — Nominal versus real money demand · relationship · D2

**Q:** If the price level P doubles while Y and i are unchanged, what happens to nominal and real money demand?

**Answer:** Nominal money demand doubles; real money demand \(MD/P\) is unchanged.

**Explanation:** People want the same purchasing power of money for the same real transactions, but twice as many dollars are required when every price has doubled.


**Common trap:** A price-level change shifts nominal MD but does not shift a real \(MD/P\) schedule expressed in purchasing-power units.


*Tags: money-demand, nominal-real*


### ch06-014 — Money demand · calculation · D2

**Q:** Suppose \(MD/P = 0.8Y - 1200i\), with i in decimal form. If \(Y=1000\) and \(i=5\%\), what is real money demand?

**Answer:** 0.8(1000) - 1200(0.05) = 740.

**Explanation:** Income contributes 800 units and the interest opportunity cost subtracts 60. If \(P=2\), nominal desired money balances would be 1480.


**Common trap:** Check whether an interest rate is written as 5 or 0.05 before substituting.


*Tags: money-demand, calculation*


### ch06-015 — Bank balance sheet · recall · D1

**Q:** On a commercial bank balance sheet, what are deposits, loans, reserves and equity?

**Answer:** Deposits are liabilities; loans and reserves are assets; equity is the residual net worth, assets minus liabilities.

**Explanation:** Depositors have claims on the bank, so deposits are amounts the bank owes. Loans are claims the bank has on borrowers. Equity absorbs losses before depositors/other creditors.


**Common trap:** From the household’s perspective a deposit is an asset; from the bank’s perspective the same deposit is a liability.


*Tags: banking, balance-sheet*


### ch06-016 — Bank balance sheet · formula · D1

**Q:** What accounting identity must a bank balance sheet satisfy?

**Answer:** Assets = liabilities + equity.

**Explanation:** Every transaction changes at least two balance-sheet entries so the identity continues to hold. This bookkeeping is central to the worksheet questions about loans and reserve transfers.


**Common trap:** A profitable bank can still face liquidity problems even while its assets exceed liabilities.


*Tags: banking, balance-sheet, formula*


### ch06-017 — Bank lending · scenario · D2

**Q:** What happens immediately when a bank grants a new $10,000 loan by crediting the borrower’s deposit account?

**Answer:** The bank records a $10,000 loan asset and a $10,000 deposit liability; the money supply rises by $10,000.

**Explanation:** Modern bank lending can create a deposit at the moment the loan is made. No pile of existing deposits has to be physically handed to the borrower first.


**Common trap:** The bank still faces funding, reserve/liquidity, capital, risk and profitability constraints; “banks create deposits” does not mean lending is unconstrained.


*Tags: banking, money-creation*


### ch06-018 — Interbank payment · scenario · D3

**Q:** A borrower spends a newly created deposit and the recipient banks elsewhere. What happens to the two banks?

**Answer:** The payer’s bank loses deposits and settlement reserves to the recipient’s bank; the recipient bank gains both.

**Explanation:** The payment transfers settlement balances between banks. The original loan remains an asset of the lending bank unless sold or repaid, so it may need to replace the lost funding/reserves.


**Common trap:** An interbank transfer redistributes reserves across banks; it does not by itself change the total settlement balances supplied to the banking system.


*Tags: banking, reserves, balance-sheet*


### ch06-019 — Bank leverage · recall · D2

**Q:** What is bank leverage conceptually?

**Answer:** Using a relatively small amount of equity to support a much larger stock of assets such as loans.

**Explanation:** Leverage magnifies returns on equity when assets perform well but also magnifies losses. The course exercises calculate a simple loan-to-equity style leverage measure in their stylised balance sheets.


**Common trap:** Different texts define a numerical leverage ratio differently (assets/equity versus loans/equity); follow the formula stated in the question/course table.


*Tags: banking, leverage*


### ch06-020 — Reserve-deposit ratio · formula · D1

**Q:** How is a bank’s reserve-deposit ratio calculated?

**Answer:** Reserves divided by deposits.

**Explanation:** It indicates the liquid settlement assets held relative to deposit liabilities. A bank with too few liquid assets can be vulnerable to sudden payment outflows even if its loan portfolio is ultimately valuable.


**Common trap:** A reserve is an asset of the commercial bank but a liability of the central bank.


*Tags: banking, reserves, formula*


### ch06-021 — Solvency versus liquidity · contrast · D2

**Q:** What is the difference between a solvent bank and a liquid bank?

**Answer:** Solvent means the value of assets is sufficient to cover liabilities; liquid means the bank can meet payments/withdrawals when due.

**Explanation:** A bank can hold sound long-term loans and be solvent but still lack enough immediately available settlement assets during a run. Conversely, temporary liquidity does not rescue a fundamentally insolvent bank forever.


**Common trap:** Do not equate inability to pay cash immediately with negative net worth.


*Tags: banking, liquidity, solvency*


### ch06-022 — Bank run · recall · D2

**Q:** What is a bank run?

**Answer:** A rapid wave of deposit withdrawals driven by fear that the bank may be unable to repay depositors.

**Explanation:** Because banks transform liquid short-term deposits into less liquid longer-term assets, even a solvent bank may struggle to meet a sufficiently large simultaneous withdrawal demand.


**Common trap:** A run can become self-reinforcing; it need not begin with the bank already having negative equity.


*Tags: banking, bank-run*


### ch06-023 — Lender of last resort · recall · D2

**Q:** How can a central bank reduce the risk that a liquidity panic destroys a solvent bank?

**Answer:** Act as lender of last resort by lending settlement liquidity against suitable collateral.

**Explanation:** Central-bank liquidity can replace funding that has suddenly fled and allow a solvent bank to meet payments while assets mature or are sold in an orderly way.


**Common trap:** Liquidity support is not intended to make fundamentally worthless assets valuable.


*Tags: banking, central-bank, stability*


### ch06-024 — Deposit insurance · recall · D2

**Q:** How can deposit insurance reduce bank-run risk?

**Answer:** By assuring protected depositors that they will be repaid even if their bank fails, reducing the incentive to rush to withdraw first.

**Explanation:** The policy can stabilise deposits, but it may also create moral hazard if banks or depositors become less sensitive to risk, which is why prudential regulation remains important.


**Common trap:** Insurance reduces run incentives; it does not eliminate all bank-risk or guarantee every liability without limit.


*Tags: banking, deposit-insurance*


### ch06-025 — Prudential regulation · recall · D2

**Q:** Why are banks subject to capital and liquidity regulation?

**Answer:** To make them better able to absorb asset losses and meet payment outflows, reducing the probability and social cost of failure.

**Explanation:** More equity provides a loss-absorbing buffer; liquidity requirements increase holdings of assets readily usable for settlement. Regulation responds to systemic externalities and safety-net moral hazard.


**Common trap:** Capital is not a vault of cash; bank capital/equity is a funding and loss-absorption concept.


*Tags: banking, regulation*


### ch06-026 — Quantity equation · formula · D1

**Q:** State the quantity equation of money.

**Answer:** MV ≡ PY.

**Explanation:** M is the money stock, V velocity, P the price level and Y real output; PY is nominal GDP. As an identity, velocity can be defined so that the equation always holds.


**Common trap:** The identity alone does not prove that changing M causes a proportional change in P.


*Tags: quantity-equation, formula*


### ch06-027 — Velocity · formula · D1

**Q:** What is the velocity of money in the quantity equation?

**Answer:** \(V = PY/M\).

**Explanation:** Velocity measures how many times, on average, a unit of money is used to support nominal expenditure during the period. Rearranging the identity gives its measured value.


**Common trap:** Velocity is not literally the observed speed of each individual banknote.


*Tags: quantity-equation, velocity*


### ch06-028 — Quantity theory · recall · D2

**Q:** What extra assumptions turn MV≡PY into the simple quantity theory prediction that money determines prices?

**Answer:** Velocity is stable (or exogenous) and real output is fixed by real-side factors in the relevant long run.

**Explanation:** Under those assumptions, a permanent proportional rise in M produces the same proportional rise in P. In growth rates, money growth in excess of real-output growth (adjusted for velocity growth) produces inflation.


**Common trap:** Do not call \(MV=PY\) is itself an accounting identity, not a causal theory; it becomes causal only after behavioural assumptions are added.


*Tags: quantity-theory, money, inflation*


### ch06-029 — Quantity theory · formula · D2

**Q:** What is the approximate growth-rate form of the quantity equation?

**Answer:** \(g_M+g_V\approx\pi+g_Y\).

**Explanation:** Taking percentage growth rates of \(MV=PY\) gives money growth plus velocity growth approximately equal to inflation plus real-output growth. If V is constant, \(\pi\)≈\(g_M-g_Y\).


**Common trap:** “Money growth equals inflation” requires both stable velocity and no real-output growth, or an explicit long-run simplification.


*Tags: quantity-theory, formula, inflation*


### ch06-030 — Quantity theory · calculation · D1

**Q:** If money grows 8%, velocity is constant and real output grows 3%, what inflation rate does simple quantity theory predict?

**Answer:** About 5%.

**Explanation:** With \(g_V=0\), \(\pi\)≈\(g_M-g_Y=8\%-3\%=5\%\). The extra nominal spending not absorbed by higher real production appears as a higher price level.


**Common trap:** Subtract real-output growth; do not simply report the money-growth rate as inflation.


*Tags: quantity-theory, calculation*


### ch06-031 — Money growth and inflation · contrast · D3

**Q:** Why can rapid money growth raise nominal GDP without immediately producing the same inflation rate in the short run?

**Answer:** Velocity and real output can change, and prices/output may respond with lags; the proportional price result is a long-run quantity-theory simplification.

**Explanation:** During slack conditions some demand increase may raise real output, and financial innovation can alter velocity. The accounting identity still holds while the causal decomposition changes.


**Common trap:** Do not use a long-run quantity-theory conclusion as an exact short-run forecasting rule.


*Tags: quantity-theory, short-run-vs-long-run*


## Chapter 7: Central Banks and Monetary Policy (28 cards)


### ch07-001 — Central bank objectives · recall · D1

**Q:** What two broad macroeconomic objectives of the RBA are emphasised in the course?

**Answer:** Price stability/inflation control and full employment, alongside the broader statutory goal of economic prosperity/welfare.

**Explanation:** For exam reasoning, monetary policy is typically represented as reacting to inflation relative to target and economic activity/output relative to potential.


**Common trap:** Do not assume the central bank directly controls inflation or employment; it controls instruments that influence financial conditions and aggregate demand.


*Tags: RBA, objectives*


### ch07-002 — Inflation target · recall · D1

**Q:** What inflation target does the course use for the RBA?

**Answer:** Consumer price inflation of 2–3% over time.

**Explanation:** The target is a range and is intended to be achieved over time, allowing flexibility in response to shocks rather than forcing inflation back instantly after every disturbance.


**Common trap:** Do not treat 2–3% as a mechanical requirement that every single quarterly observation lie inside the band.


*Tags: RBA, inflation-target, course-fact*


### ch07-003 — Headline versus underlying inflation · contrast · D2

**Q:** Why might a central bank examine underlying/core inflation as well as headline CPI inflation?

**Answer:** To distinguish persistent broad inflation pressure from temporary or unusually volatile price movements.

**Explanation:** Headline inflation measures the actual CPI change faced by consumers, while underlying measures can help assess the trend relevant for medium-term policy.


**Common trap:** Underlying inflation is an analytical measure, not a substitute for the target variable unless the course explicitly says so.


*Tags: inflation, monetary-policy*


### ch07-004 — Instrument versus target · contrast · D1

**Q:** What is the difference between a monetary-policy instrument and an ultimate target?

**Answer:** The instrument is the variable the central bank can closely control, such as the cash rate; targets are outcomes such as inflation and employment/output.

**Explanation:** The RBA cannot set GDP or inflation by decree. It changes its operating instrument, which influences other interest rates, spending, exchange rates and expectations.


**Common trap:** Do not list the inflation rate itself as the cash-market operating instrument.


*Tags: monetary-policy, instrument*


### ch07-005 — Cash rate · recall · D1

**Q:** What is the cash rate in the Australian monetary system?

**Answer:** The overnight interest rate on unsecured loans of Exchange Settlement balances between financial institutions.

**Explanation:** Banks use ES balances to settle obligations with one another. The RBA implements monetary policy by keeping the market overnight rate close to its target.


**Common trap:** The cash rate is not the same thing as a household mortgage rate, although it influences broader rates.


*Tags: RBA, cash-rate, ESA*


### ch07-006 — Exchange Settlement balances · recall · D2

**Q:** What are Exchange Settlement (ES) balances?

**Answer:** Deposits that eligible financial institutions hold at the RBA and use to settle interbank payments.

**Explanation:** They are reserve/settlement assets for commercial banks and liabilities of the RBA. Transactions with the government/RBA can change their total supply, whereas ordinary interbank transfers mostly redistribute them.


**Common trap:** ES balances are not household transaction deposits and are not counted as extra public money on top of deposits.


*Tags: RBA, ESA, reserves*


### ch07-007 — Cash-rate corridor · recall · D2

**Q:** How does a central-bank interest-rate corridor help pin down the overnight cash rate?

**Answer:** A standing deposit/remuneration rate creates a floor and a standing lending facility creates a ceiling, so banks have little reason to trade reserves outside the corridor.

**Explanation:** The policy target lies within the corridor. If market rates fall below what banks can earn safely at the central bank, lenders withdraw; if rates rise above central-bank borrowing cost, borrowers use the facility instead.


**Common trap:** Use the corridor widths specified in the question: historical/textbook examples need not match the lecture’s current operational spread.


*Tags: RBA, corridor, cash-rate*


### ch07-008 — Corridor calculation · calculation · D1

**Q:** A hypothetical corridor is 50 basis points wide and centred on a 2.00% target. What are the floor and ceiling?

**Answer:** 1.75% and 2.25%.

**Explanation:** A total width of 0.50 percentage points centred on the target means 0.25 percentage points on each side. This is the textbook review example.


**Common trap:** Fifty basis points = 0.50 percentage points, not 50%.


*Tags: cash-rate, calculation*


### ch07-009 — Reserve demand · relationship · D2

**Q:** Why is the banking system’s demand for ES balances downward sloping in the overnight interest rate?

**Answer:** Holding more settlement balances becomes more costly relative to lending them when the market interest rate is high, so desired balances are lower.

**Explanation:** Banks still need liquidity for payments, but the opportunity cost varies with the overnight rate. A stylised reserve-demand schedule is therefore downward sloping.


**Common trap:** Do not confuse system-wide reserve demand with an individual bank’s desire to receive a payment transfer.


*Tags: ESA, money-market, demand*


### ch07-010 — Reserve demand · calculation · D2

**Q:** If reserve demand is \(R^d=10-2.5i\), with \(i\) measured in percentage points, how many reserves are demanded at \(i=2\)?

**Answer:** 5 units.

**Explanation:** Substitute \(i=2: 10-2.5(2)=5\). This is the style used in the Chapter 7 review to connect a target cash rate with the required reserve supply.


**Common trap:** Check the unit convention: this equation uses 2 for 2%, unlike formulas that use 0.02.


*Tags: ESA, calculation*


### ch07-011 — Open-market purchase · scenario · D1

**Q:** What does an RBA open-market purchase of securities do to ES balances and, all else equal, the overnight cash rate?

**Answer:** It adds ES balances/reserves and puts downward pressure on the cash rate.

**Explanation:** The RBA pays for the security by crediting settlement balances. Greater reserve supply makes overnight funds less scarce at the previous rate.


**Common trap:** Purchase by the central bank means it buys securities and injects settlement funds; do not reverse the direction.


*Tags: OMO, cash-rate, high-yield*


### ch07-012 — Open-market sale · scenario · D1

**Q:** What does an RBA open-market sale of securities do to ES balances and the cash rate, all else equal?

**Answer:** It drains ES balances/reserves and puts upward pressure on the cash rate.

**Explanation:** Buyers pay the RBA, reducing balances in the settlement system. Scarcer overnight funds raise the market rate toward the desired target/corridor.


**Common trap:** An RBA sale is contractionary for settlement liquidity; a commercial bank selling a security to another commercial bank only redistributes balances.


*Tags: OMO, cash-rate, high-yield*


### ch07-013 — Government payments and ESAs · scenario · D2

**Q:** The government makes pension payments from its RBA account into households’ commercial-bank deposits. What happens to total ES balances?

**Answer:** They increase.

**Explanation:** The RBA credits the recipient banks’ settlement accounts as the government’s account is debited. This injects settlement balances into the banking system unless offset by RBA liquidity operations.


**Common trap:** Although the households receive deposits, the worksheet’s cash-market question is specifically about the banks’ ES balances.


*Tags: ESA, government-transaction*


### ch07-014 — Interbank security trade · scenario · D2

**Q:** Bank A buys a security from Bank B and settles through their ES accounts. What happens to total ES balances in the banking system?

**Answer:** The total is unchanged; ES balances are transferred from A to B.

**Explanation:** An interbank private transaction changes the distribution of central-bank balances, not their aggregate quantity. The RBA/government sector is needed to inject or withdraw the system total.


**Common trap:** Do not add the receiving bank’s gain without subtracting the paying bank’s loss.


*Tags: ESA, interbank, balance-sheet*


### ch07-015 — Cash-rate target increase · scenario · D2

**Q:** How can the RBA implement a higher cash-rate target in the stylised reserve-market diagram?

**Answer:** Tighten settlement liquidity/shift the relevant operating corridor upward so the equilibrium overnight rate rises to the new target.

**Explanation:** Operational details vary with the corridor system, but the economic direction is contractionary: the overnight price of funds rises and propagates to broader interest rates.


**Common trap:** Do not say a higher target requires permanently flooding the system with reserves at the old corridor.


*Tags: RBA, cash-rate, policy*


### ch07-016 — Monetary transmission · sequence · D2

**Q:** Give the core interest-rate channel from tighter monetary policy to aggregate demand.

**Answer:** \[\text{cash rate}\uparrow\to\text{broader nominal interest rates}\uparrow\to\text{expected real interest rates}\uparrow\to\text{interest-sensitive }C\text{ and }I\downarrow\to PAE/AD\downarrow\to\text{output pressure}\downarrow\]

**Explanation:** Other channels can include exchange rates, asset prices, credit and expectations, but the course’s macro model foregrounds the real interest-rate effect on consumption and investment.


**Common trap:** The spending decision depends primarily on expected real borrowing/return conditions, not the nominal rate in isolation.


*Tags: monetary-transmission, sequence*


### ch07-017 — Real-rate channel · formula · D2

**Q:** Why can a nominal-rate change alter the expected real interest rate in the short run?

**Answer:** Because expected real \(r\approx i-\pi^e\), and inflation expectations need not jump one-for-one with a policy-rate change.

**Explanation:** If expected inflation is temporarily sticky, a higher nominal policy/bank rate translates into a higher expected real rate and suppresses interest-sensitive spending.


**Common trap:** If nominal rates rise only because expected inflation rose equally, the real rate may not change.


*Tags: real-interest-rate, monetary-policy*


### ch07-018 — Yield curve · recall · D2

**Q:** What is the yield curve?

**Answer:** A relationship between yields/interest rates and maturity for otherwise comparable debt securities.

**Explanation:** Its shape reflects expectations of future short-term rates plus term/risk premia. Monetary policy influences short rates directly and longer rates partly through expectations of the future policy path.


**Common trap:** Comparing yields across very different default risks confounds maturity with credit risk.


*Tags: yield-curve, bonds*


### ch07-019 — Expectations hypothesis · formula · D3

**Q:** Under the simplified expectations hypothesis with no term premium, how is a two-year interest rate related to current and expected one-year rates?

**Answer:** \((1+i_{0,2})^2=(1+i_{0,1})(1+E[i_{1,2}])\); approximately, the two-year rate is the average of the two one-year rates.

**Explanation:** Investors should be indifferent between locking in two years and rolling over one-year securities if expected compounded returns are equal and there is no term premium.


**Common trap:** Use compounding for exact calculations; the arithmetic average is an approximation for modest rates.


*Tags: yield-curve, expectations-hypothesis, formula*


### ch07-020 — Expectations hypothesis · calculation · D2

**Q:** If the current one-year rate is 2% and the expected one-year rate next year is 4%, what is the approximate two-year annual rate under the expectations hypothesis?

**Answer:** About 3%.

**Explanation:** The approximation averages expected short rates: \((2\%+4\%)/2=3\%\). Exactly, solve \((1+i_2)^2=1.02\times1.04\), giving just under 3\%.


**Common trap:** Do not add the two annual rates to get 6%; the two-year quote is an annualised rate.


*Tags: yield-curve, calculation*


### ch07-021 — Term premium · recall · D3

**Q:** Why can a long-term interest rate differ from the average expected future short rates?

**Answer:** Investors may require a term/risk premium for holding longer-maturity securities.

**Explanation:** Long bonds expose holders to interest-rate and inflation uncertainty over a longer horizon. The simple expectations hypothesis abstracts from this premium; richer versions add it.


**Common trap:** A steep yield curve is not mechanically proof that only future short rates will rise; term premia can move too.


*Tags: yield-curve, term-premium*


### ch07-022 — Policy rule · recall · D2

**Q:** What is the intuition of a Taylor-style monetary-policy rule?

**Answer:** Raise the policy nominal interest rate when inflation is above target and/or output is above potential, and lower it when inflation is below target or output is weak.

**Explanation:** A systematic rule summarises stabilising policy behaviour: excessive inflation/activity calls for tighter financial conditions; weak inflation/activity calls for easier conditions.


**Common trap:** A Taylor rule is a descriptive/normative benchmark, not a claim that the RBA mechanically follows one exact equation.


*Tags: Taylor-rule, policy-rule*


### ch07-023 — Taylor rule · calculation · D2

**Q:** Using \(i=4+1.5(\pi-\pi^T)+0.5\,\text{output gap}\), what is i if \(\pi=4\%\), \(\pi^T=2\%\) and the output gap is +2%?

**Answer:** 4 + 1.5(2) + 0.5(2) = 8%.

**Explanation:** Both inflation and output are above desired levels, so the rule prescribes a substantially higher nominal rate than its 4% baseline.


**Common trap:** Use the inflation gap \(\pi-\pi^T\), not \(\pi\) itself in the response term.


*Tags: Taylor-rule, calculation*


### ch07-024 — Taylor principle · recall · D3

**Q:** Why should a stabilising policy rule often raise the nominal interest rate by more than one-for-one when inflation rises persistently?

**Answer:** So that the real interest rate rises, making policy genuinely contractionary rather than allowing higher inflation to lower the real rate.

**Explanation:** If i rose less than inflation expectations, \(r\approx i-\pi^e\) could fall, stimulating demand and amplifying inflation. A coefficient on inflation above one embodies the Taylor principle.


**Common trap:** The rule concerns the response of the nominal rate to inflation; the objective is the direction of the real rate.


*Tags: Taylor-rule, real-interest-rate*


### ch07-025 — Zero lower bound · recall · D2

**Q:** What problem does the effective lower bound on nominal interest rates create in a severe recession?

**Answer:** The central bank may be unable to cut its normal short-term nominal-rate instrument far enough to generate the desired low/negative real rate.

**Explanation:** If inflation expectations are low or negative, \(r=i-\pi^e\) can remain too high even when i is near zero. This motivates unconventional policy or complementary fiscal support in severe downturns.


**Common trap:** A zero nominal rate does not imply a zero real rate.


*Tags: zero-lower-bound, monetary-policy*


### ch07-026 — Policy reaction function · formula · D2

**Q:** What is a simple monetary-policy reaction function (PRF) in the course AD model?

**Answer:** A rule in which the real interest rate rises with inflation, e.g. \(r=\bar r+\gamma\pi\) (or equivalently with the inflation gap), with \(\gamma>0\).

**Explanation:** The PRF compresses the central bank’s behaviour into the real rate used in spending decisions. Higher inflation elicits tighter real monetary conditions.


**Common trap:** This is not the money-demand curve; it maps inflation into the policy-relevant real interest rate.


*Tags: PRF, formula, AD-AS*


### ch07-027 — PRF shift · scenario · D1

**Q:** What does an upward exogenous shift of the PRF mean?

**Answer:** Contractionary monetary policy: a higher real interest rate at every given inflation rate.

**Explanation:** Because C and I fall at a given \(\pi\), planned expenditure and aggregate demand shift left. A downward PRF shift is expansionary.


**Common trap:** A movement upward along an unchanged PRF caused by higher inflation is not the same as an exogenous policy shift.


*Tags: PRF, shift-vs-movement, high-yield*


### ch07-028 — Inflation target and flexibility · recall · D3

**Q:** Why does flexible inflation targeting not require the central bank to eliminate every inflation deviation immediately?

**Answer:** Rapidly offsetting supply shocks can impose unnecessarily large output/employment costs, so policy can return inflation toward target over time while also stabilising activity.

**Explanation:** The dual concern for inflation and real activity is why policy responses depend on the source and persistence of a shock, not just the current CPI number.


**Common trap:** Temporary headline inflation above target does not mechanically imply the largest possible rate rise.


*Tags: inflation-target, policy-tradeoff*


## Chapter 8: Aggregate Demand and Aggregate Supply (32 cards)


### ch08-001 — AD model building blocks · recall · D2

**Q:** What three relationships are combined to derive the course’s aggregate-demand (AD) curve?

**Answer:** Planned aggregate expenditure/equilibrium output, the negative effect of the real interest rate on C and I, and the monetary-policy reaction function linking inflation to the real interest rate.

**Explanation:** Inflation affects the real policy rate through the PRF; the real rate affects planned expenditure; equilibrium expenditure determines output. Combining them gives Y as a decreasing function of inflation.


**Common trap:** The course AD curve is not introduced primarily via a fixed nominal money supply and real balances; it is built around an interest-rate-setting central bank.


*Tags: AD, derivation*


### ch08-002 — Interest-sensitive spending · relationship · D1

**Q:** How does a higher real interest rate affect consumption and planned investment in the Chapter 8 model?

**Answer:** It reduces interest-sensitive consumption and investment, lowering PAE.

**Explanation:** Higher real borrowing costs discourage durable consumption/investment, while a higher return to saving can postpone consumption. Investment projects also fail the profitability hurdle as user cost rises.


**Common trap:** Government purchases and exports are often treated as autonomous in the basic derivation unless the question says otherwise.


*Tags: AD, real-interest-rate*


### ch08-003 — PAE with real rate · calculation · D1

**Q:** If \(C=200+0.6Y-20r\) and \(I=80-10r\), with no other sectors, what is PAE?

**Answer:** \(PAE = 280 + 0.6Y - 30r\).

**Explanation:** Add C and I: autonomous expenditure is 280, induced expenditure is \(0.6Y\), and total interest sensitivity is \(-20r-10r=-30r\).


**Common trap:** Combine the two r coefficients; do not drop the investment response.


*Tags: PAE, real-interest-rate, calculation*


### ch08-004 — Output and real rate · formula · D2

**Q:** Using \(PAE=280+0.6Y-30r\), what equilibrium-output relation follows?

**Answer:** \(Y = 700 - 75r\).

**Explanation:** Set \(Y=PAE\): \(0.4Y=280-30r\), then divide by 0.4. This is the intermediate “IS-like” relation used before substituting the policy reaction function.


**Common trap:** Equilibrium requires \(Y=PAE\), not merely reading 280-30r as output.


*Tags: AD, derivation, calculation*


### ch08-005 — Derive AD · calculation · D2

**Q:** If equilibrium output is \(Y=700-75r\) and the PRF is \(r=1.5+0.5\pi\), what is the AD curve?

**Answer:** \(Y=587.5-37.5\pi\), approximately \(Y=587-37.5\pi\) if rounded as in course material.

**Explanation:** Substitute the PRF: \(Y=700-75(1.5+0.5\pi)=700-112.5-37.5\pi\). The negative coefficient on inflation is the AD slope.


**Common trap:** Do not substitute a nominal rate if the spending equations are written in terms of the real rate.


*Tags: AD, derivation, calculation*


### ch08-006 — AD slope · recall · D1

**Q:** Why does the course’s AD curve slope downward in inflation-output space?

**Answer:** Higher inflation prompts the central bank to raise the real interest rate; higher r lowers C and I; lower PAE reduces equilibrium output.

**Explanation:** The causal chain is \(\pi\)↑ → r↑ → interest-sensitive spending↓ → PAE↓ → Y↓. This is the exact intuition highlighted in Worksheet 7.


**Common trap:** Do not give the standard micro demand-law explanation “higher prices reduce quantity demanded”; AD is an economy-wide policy/spending relationship.


*Tags: AD, high-yield, sequence*


### ch08-007 — Movement along AD · scenario · D1

**Q:** What causes a movement along an unchanged AD curve in this model?

**Answer:** A change in inflation that induces the corresponding real-rate and output response through an unchanged PRF and unchanged autonomous spending.

**Explanation:** Inflation is an axis variable. Changing it traces out different combinations of \(\pi\) and Y consistent with the same underlying behavioural equations.


**Common trap:** A change in G, autonomous consumption, exports or the PRF shifts AD rather than moving along it.


*Tags: AD, shift-vs-movement*


### ch08-008 — AD shift · scenario · D1

**Q:** What happens to AD if autonomous exports fall?

**Answer:** AD shifts left.

**Explanation:** At every inflation rate, the same PRF sets the same real rate, but lower exports reduce autonomous PAE and equilibrium output. Worksheet 7 uses this as a shift question.


**Common trap:** Because inflation has not caused the change, do not call it a movement up the AD curve.


*Tags: AD, shift, exports*


### ch08-009 — AD shift · scenario · D2

**Q:** What happens to AD after an exogenous upward shift in the PRF?

**Answer:** AD shifts left.

**Explanation:** At every inflation rate the central bank now sets a higher real interest rate. Consumption and investment are lower, so equilibrium output is lower at every \(\pi\).


**Common trap:** An upward PRF shift is contractionary; it is easy to reverse this because the PRF itself moves upward while AD moves left.


*Tags: AD, monetary-policy, shift*


### ch08-010 — AD shift · scenario · D2

**Q:** Name common changes that shift AD to the right.

**Answer:** Higher autonomous C, I, G or X; lower autonomous taxes; or an exogenous expansionary/downward PRF shift.

**Explanation:** Anything that raises equilibrium planned spending at a given inflation rate shifts AD right. The exact list depends on which variables are included in the model in the question.


**Common trap:** A lower current inflation rate by itself is a movement along AD, not a rightward shift.


*Tags: AD, shift*


### ch08-011 — Output gap · formula · D1

**Q:** How is the output gap defined?

**Answer:** Actual output minus potential output, often expressed as a percentage of potential: \(\frac{Y-Y^*}{Y^*}\times100\%\).

**Explanation:** Y>Y* is an expansionary/positive gap; Y<Y* is a contractionary/negative gap. The gap drives inflation pressure in the aggregate-supply relationship.


**Common trap:** Potential output is sustainable/full-employment output, not the absolute physical maximum the economy could produce for a moment.


*Tags: output-gap, formula*


### ch08-012 — Aggregate supply · recall · D2

**Q:** What variables drive current inflation in the course’s short-run aggregate-supply/inflation equation?

**Answer:** Expected inflation, the output gap and any inflation/supply shock.

**Explanation:** A generic form is \(\pi=\pi^e+\lambda\,\text{output gap}+\text{supply shock}\), with \(\lambda>0\). Above-potential output puts upward pressure on inflation; below-potential output puts downward pressure.


**Common trap:** The exact coefficients/sign convention can vary; preserve the intuition and use the equation supplied in a calculation.


*Tags: AS, inflation, formula*


### ch08-013 — Adaptive expectations · recall · D1

**Q:** What is adaptive inflation expectation formation in the simplest course model?

**Answer:** Expected current inflation is based on recently observed inflation, often \(\pi^e_t = \pi_{t-1}\).

**Explanation:** This creates inflation persistence: today’s inflation becomes the benchmark for next period’s wage/price setting unless an output gap or shock moves it.


**Common trap:** Adaptive expectations are backward-looking, unlike fully forward-looking rational expectations.


*Tags: AS, expectations*


### ch08-014 — Short-run AS shape · recall · D2

**Q:** Why can the course depict very short-run AS as horizontal at inherited inflation?

**Answer:** With sticky prices/wages and adaptive expectations fixed from the previous period, firms can initially meet demand changes at roughly the preset inflation rate.

**Explanation:** A demand shift therefore changes output first. Persistent output gaps then change inflation in subsequent periods, shifting the short-run inflation line.


**Common trap:** Do not import a differently drawn upward-sloping SRAS from another textbook if the question uses the course’s lagged-inflation diagram.


*Tags: AS, course-convention, short-run*


### ch08-015 — Inflation dynamics · scenario · D1

**Q:** If Y>Y* and there is no favourable supply shock, what happens to inflation over time?

**Answer:** Inflation tends to rise.

**Explanation:** An expansionary output gap creates excess demand and pressure on wages/prices. With adaptive expectations, the higher inflation can then become embedded in the next period’s expected inflation.


**Common trap:** A positive output gap is not synonymous with a high price level; it creates a change in inflation dynamics.


*Tags: AS, output-gap, inflation*


### ch08-016 — Inflation dynamics · scenario · D1

**Q:** If Y<Y* and there is no adverse supply shock, what happens to inflation over time?

**Answer:** Inflation tends to fall.

**Explanation:** A contractionary gap weakens wage and price pressure. Under adaptive expectations, lower realised inflation feeds into lower expected inflation in later periods.


**Common trap:** Falling inflation (disinflation) does not necessarily mean the price level itself is falling; that requires negative inflation.


*Tags: AS, output-gap, inflation*


### ch08-017 — Long-run equilibrium · recall · D2

**Q:** What condition characterises long-run macroeconomic equilibrium in the basic AD-AS model?

**Answer:** Output equals potential output, \(Y=Y^*\), so there is no output-gap pressure causing inflation to keep changing.

**Explanation:** At \(Y=Y^*\), expected and actual inflation can be consistent, conditional on no new supply shock. AD determines which inflation rate is compatible with potential output and the policy regime.


**Common trap:** Long-run equilibrium does not require zero inflation; it can occur at the central bank’s positive target.


*Tags: AD-AS, long-run*


### ch08-018 — Expansionary AD shock · sequence · D3

**Q:** Starting from long-run equilibrium, what is the typical adjustment after a persistent positive AD shock?

**Answer:** Initially Y rises above Y* at roughly inherited inflation; the positive gap then raises inflation; policy real rates rise and/or short-run AS adjusts until Y returns to Y*, generally at a higher inflation rate if the AD shift persists.

**Explanation:** The key distinction is short-run real effects versus long-run restoration of potential output. Inflation adjustment eliminates the sustained output gap.


**Common trap:** Do not leave the economy permanently above potential just because the initial AD shift was permanent.


*Tags: AD-AS, demand-shock, sequence*


### ch08-019 — Negative AD shock · sequence · D3

**Q:** Starting from long-run equilibrium, what is the typical adjustment after a persistent negative AD shock?

**Answer:** Initially Y falls below Y*; the contractionary gap causes inflation to decline; easier real monetary conditions and inflation adjustment move output back toward Y*, at a lower inflation rate if the AD shift persists.

**Explanation:** Self-correction operates through the effect of the output gap on inflation and the monetary-policy response to inflation.


**Common trap:** The short-run recession can still be costly even if the model eventually self-corrects.


*Tags: AD-AS, demand-shock, sequence*


### ch08-020 — Demand stabilisation · scenario · D1

**Q:** How can expansionary monetary policy respond to a negative AD shock/recession?

**Answer:** Shift the PRF downward, lowering the real interest rate at each inflation rate and shifting AD right.

**Explanation:** Lower real rates support \(C\) and \(I\), reducing the contractionary output gap. Fiscal expansion can also shift autonomous \(PAE/AD\) right.


**Common trap:** A rate cut is expansionary because it lowers r; an upward PRF shift would worsen the contraction.


*Tags: AD-AS, stabilisation, monetary-policy*


### ch08-021 — Self-correction versus policy · contrast · D2

**Q:** Why might policymakers still stabilise a demand shock if the economy eventually self-corrects?

**Answer:** Because self-correction can be slow, so unemployment and lost output can persist while inflation/expectations adjust.

**Explanation:** Stabilisation trades off the risks of policy lags, measurement error and side effects against the cost of leaving a large output gap unaddressed.


**Common trap:** “Eventually returns to Y*” does not imply the transition has zero welfare cost.


*Tags: AD-AS, stabilisation, lags*


### ch08-022 — Adverse supply shock · scenario · D2

**Q:** What is an adverse inflation/supply shock, and what does it do in the short run?

**Answer:** A shock that raises inflation at a given output gap, such as a sharp input-cost increase; it shifts short-run AS/inflation upward and can combine higher inflation with lower output.

**Explanation:** Unlike a pure demand shock, it creates a policy trade-off: contracting AD fights inflation but worsens output, while expansion supports output but accommodates more inflation.


**Common trap:** Do not automatically prescribe expansionary policy merely because output falls; first identify whether the disturbance is demand-side or supply-side.


*Tags: AS, supply-shock, stagflation*


### ch08-023 — Favourable supply shock · scenario · D2

**Q:** What does a favourable supply/inflation shock do?

**Answer:** It lowers inflation pressure at a given output gap, permitting lower inflation and/or stronger output in the short run.

**Explanation:** Examples can include temporary falls in important input prices or improvements that reduce production costs. Policy may be able to tolerate stronger demand without exceeding inflation objectives.


**Common trap:** A temporary favourable cost shock is not the same as a permanent rise in potential output/productivity.


*Tags: AS, supply-shock*


### ch08-024 — Supply-shock policy tradeoff · recall · D2

**Q:** Why is an adverse supply shock harder for a central bank to stabilise than a pure demand shock?

**Answer:** Because inflation and output move in undesirable opposite directions: inflation rises while output weakens.

**Explanation:** A tighter policy lowers inflation but deepens the negative output effect; easier policy supports output but allows more inflation. Flexible inflation targeting balances the two objectives over time.


**Common trap:** For a negative demand shock, both lower output and lower inflation generally call for easing; the objectives are not in conflict.


*Tags: AS, policy-tradeoff*


### ch08-025 — Accommodating inflation · recall · D3

**Q:** What does it mean to “accommodate” an adverse inflation shock?

**Answer:** Allow demand/policy to support output rather than fully offsetting the shock’s inflation effect, accepting more inflation at least temporarily.

**Explanation:** Accommodation can reduce the recessionary cost of a supply shock but risks entrenching higher inflation expectations if continued too long.


**Common trap:** Accommodation is not the same as eliminating the original supply problem.


*Tags: AS, monetary-policy, accommodation*


### ch08-026 — Potential-output shock · scenario · D3

**Q:** What happens when potential output Y* permanently falls, holding AD initially unchanged?

**Answer:** The long-run sustainable output benchmark shifts left/down; the old level of Y now represents a positive output gap, creating upward inflation pressure until output adjusts to the new lower Y*.

**Explanation:** This is different from a temporary cost shock because productive capacity itself has changed. Long-run output cannot be restored by demand stimulus alone.


**Common trap:** Trying to hold Y permanently at the old potential with demand expansion would generate continuing inflation pressure.


*Tags: potential-output, supply-shock, long-run*


### ch08-027 — Potential-output improvement · scenario · D2

**Q:** How does a permanent rise in productivity/potential output affect the AD-AS economy?

**Answer:** Y* rises, allowing a higher sustainable level of real output; at the old demand position there may initially be a contractionary gap and downward inflation pressure.

**Explanation:** Over time monetary/demand conditions can accommodate the greater productive capacity without creating an inflationary gap.


**Common trap:** A supply-side increase in Y* is not itself a rightward shift of the AD curve.


*Tags: potential-output, productivity*


### ch08-028 — Inflation target change · scenario · D3

**Q:** In the course policy model, what is the long-run implication of a credible permanently higher inflation target, holding real-side potential unchanged?

**Answer:** Long-run output remains Y*, but equilibrium inflation can be higher; monetary policy is easier relative to the old target during the transition.

**Explanation:** A nominal inflation objective does not permanently raise the economy’s productive capacity. Any temporary output effect disappears once the output gap closes.


**Common trap:** Do not claim a permanently higher target permanently raises real GDP above potential.


*Tags: inflation-target, long-run-neutrality*


### ch08-029 — Fiscal timing error · scenario · D2

**Q:** Why can a delayed discretionary tax cut announced in a recession become destabilising?

**Answer:** If it takes effect after output has already recovered to or above potential, it shifts AD right when expansion is no longer needed, creating an expansionary gap/inflation pressure.

**Explanation:** This is a classic policy-lag MCQ: evaluate the state of the economy when the policy takes effect, not just when it was announced.


**Common trap:** Do not label a policy stabilising solely from its intended original purpose.


*Tags: fiscal-policy, lags, AD-AS*


### ch08-030 — Global financial crisis · recall · D2

**Q:** How is a financial crisis represented in the basic AD framework when it depresses confidence, credit and private spending?

**Answer:** As a leftward AD shift through lower autonomous consumption/investment and/or tighter effective financial conditions.

**Explanation:** Falling wealth, uncertainty and impaired credit reduce planned private demand. Monetary and fiscal easing can counter part of the resulting contractionary gap.


**Common trap:** The banking shock originates in finance but its macro demand effect is still represented as weaker spending/AD.


*Tags: financial-crisis, AD*


### ch08-031 — AD equation · relationship · D3

**Q:** If the sensitivity of spending to the real interest rate becomes larger, what happens to the absolute slope coefficient in an equation written \(Y = a - b\pi\), assuming the PRF response is unchanged?

**Answer:** b becomes larger: output responds more strongly to inflation-induced real-rate changes.

**Explanation:** Stronger interest sensitivity amplifies the chain \(\pi\)→r→C/I→Y. In a graph with inflation vertical and output horizontal, be careful translating the algebraic coefficient into visual “steepness.”


**Common trap:** Do not rely on the word “steeper” without checking which variable is on which axis; use the equation’s comparative statics.


*Tags: AD, comparative-statics*


### ch08-032 — PRF response strength · relationship · D3

**Q:** If the central bank reacts more strongly to inflation (larger \(\gamma\) in \(r=\bar r+\gamma\pi\)), how does output respond to a given inflation change along AD?

**Answer:** More strongly in the negative direction.

**Explanation:** A larger \(\gamma\) produces a larger real-rate increase for the same rise in inflation, hence a larger reduction in interest-sensitive spending and equilibrium output.


**Common trap:** Again distinguish the coefficient in Y(\(\pi\)) from the visual slope if axes are reversed.


*Tags: AD, PRF, comparative-statics*


## Chapter 9: International Macroeconomics and Exchange Rates (40 cards)


### ch09-001 — Balance of payments · recall · D1

**Q:** What is the balance of payments (BOP)?

**Answer:** A set of accounts recording economic transactions between residents of a country and the rest of the world over a period.

**Explanation:** The BOP is a flow accounting system. Transactions are grouped mainly into the current account and the capital/financial account, with double-entry bookkeeping ensuring offsetting entries overall.


**Common trap:** Do not confuse the BOP with the stock of foreign assets and liabilities at one point in time.


*Tags: BOP, definition*


### ch09-002 — Current account · recall · D1

**Q:** What belongs in the current account?

**Answer:** Trade in goods and services, net primary income from abroad, and net secondary income/current transfers.

**Explanation:** A compact expression is \(CA=\text{exports}-\text{imports}+\text{net primary income}+\text{net secondary income}\). The trade balance is only one component of the current account.


**Common trap:** Buying a foreign share is a financial-account transaction, not an import of a good or service.


*Tags: BOP, current-account*


### ch09-003 — Trade balance · formula · D1

**Q:** How is the trade balance (net exports) defined?

**Answer:** \(NX = X - M\).

**Explanation:** Exports are domestic production sold abroad; imports are foreign production purchased domestically. A trade surplus has X>M and a trade deficit has X<M.


**Common trap:** The current account can differ from NX because it also includes cross-border income and transfers.


*Tags: BOP, trade-balance, formula*


### ch09-004 — Primary income · recall · D2

**Q:** Give examples of primary-income flows in the current account.

**Answer:** Wages earned across borders and investment income such as interest, dividends or profits received from foreign assets or paid to foreign owners.

**Explanation:** Primary income is compensation for supplying labour or capital. It helps explain why GDP (location of production) and national income/GNI (income of residents) can differ.


**Common trap:** A purchase of the underlying share or bond is financial account; the dividend/interest it later pays is primary income in the current account.


*Tags: BOP, primary-income*


### ch09-005 — Secondary income · recall · D2

**Q:** What are secondary-income/current-transfer flows in the current account?

**Answer:** One-way current transfers where no good, service or factor income is received in return, such as some remittances or international aid transfers.

**Explanation:** These redistribute income internationally without being payment for current production or factor services.


**Common trap:** Do not classify a payment for imported consulting as a transfer; it is an import of a service.


*Tags: BOP, secondary-income*


### ch09-006 — Financial account · recall · D2

**Q:** What does the financial account record?

**Answer:** Cross-border transactions in financial assets and liabilities, such as purchases/sales of shares, bonds, deposits, loans and direct investment.

**Explanation:** A current-account deficit is financed by a net financial inflow: foreigners acquire more claims on the domestic economy and/or residents reduce foreign claims.


**Common trap:** The exact credit/debit sign conventions can be easy to reverse; focus first on whether capital is flowing into or out of the country.


*Tags: BOP, financial-account*


### ch09-007 — BOP identity · formula · D1

**Q:** Ignoring statistical discrepancy, what accounting identity links the current account to the capital/financial account?

**Answer:** Their balances sum to zero under the course’s sign convention: CA + \(KFA = 0\).

**Explanation:** Every international transaction creates offsetting entries. If a country buys more current goods/services/income than it receives, it must finance that gap by net asset sales/borrowing from abroad.


**Common trap:** A current-account deficit does not make the entire BOP “out of balance”; the financial side provides the counterpart.


*Tags: BOP, identity, high-yield*


### ch09-008 — BOP transaction · scenario · D2

**Q:** An Australian buys a US-produced laptop and pays using an Australian bank claim that ultimately becomes a US resident’s Australian financial asset. What are the broad BOP entries?

**Answer:** An Australian current-account debit/import and an offsetting financial inflow/financial-account entry.

**Explanation:** The real resource flows into Australia while a financial claim flows the other way. Double-entry accounting records both sides of the same transaction.


**Common trap:** Do not record only the import and conclude the BOP fails to balance.


*Tags: BOP, transaction, classification*


### ch09-009 — BOP transaction · scenario · D1

**Q:** An Australian resident buys newly issued shares in a foreign company. Is this a current-account import?

**Answer:** No. It is acquisition of a foreign financial asset, recorded in the financial account.

**Explanation:** No currently produced good or service crosses the border merely because ownership of a financial claim changes. Any later dividends would be primary income.


**Common trap:** Financial assets are not counted as goods/services imports in GDP or the current account.


*Tags: BOP, financial-account, classification*


### ch09-010 — Open-economy saving identity · formula · D1

**Q:** What identity links national saving, investment and the current account?

**Answer:** \(CA = S - I\), equivalently \(S = I + CA\).

**Explanation:** If national saving exceeds domestic investment, the excess is lent/invested abroad and the country runs a current-account surplus. If investment exceeds saving, foreign saving finances the gap and CA is negative.


**Common trap:** In a closed economy \(S=I\); in an open economy domestic investment need not equal national saving.


*Tags: open-economy, saving-investment, formula*


### ch09-011 — Current-account deficit · recall · D2

**Q:** What does a current-account deficit imply about national saving and domestic investment?

**Answer:** S < I; the economy uses net foreign saving/capital inflow to finance part of domestic spending/investment.

**Explanation:** The deficit has an offsetting financial inflow. Whether this is concerning depends on why it arises and whether the foreign-financed spending generates future capacity to service liabilities.


**Common trap:** A current-account deficit is not mechanically “bad”; an investment boom can create one for productive reasons.


*Tags: current-account, saving-investment*


### ch09-012 — Current-account surplus · recall · D2

**Q:** What does a current-account surplus imply about S and I?

**Answer:** S > I; the country is a net lender/acquirer of financial claims on the rest of the world.

**Explanation:** Domestic saving not used for domestic investment finances investment or borrowing abroad.


**Common trap:** A surplus is not automatically evidence of stronger welfare; it can also reflect unusually weak domestic investment.


*Tags: current-account, saving-investment*


### ch09-013 — Small open economy · recall · D2

**Q:** What is the key interest-rate assumption for a small open economy with high capital mobility?

**Answer:** It takes the world real interest rate r* as given, so the domestic real rate is pinned close to r*.

**Explanation:** Because the country is too small to move global financial prices, saving and investment can differ domestically, with net capital flows bridging the gap.


**Common trap:** Do not force domestic S and I to intersect by changing r as in the closed-economy Chapter 3 model.


*Tags: small-open-economy, world-interest-rate*


### ch09-014 — Fiscal deficit in small open economy · scenario · D3

**Q:** In the stylised small open economy, what does a government budget deficit that lowers national saving tend to do if r remains fixed at r*?

**Answer:** It worsens the current account/increases net foreign borrowing rather than necessarily crowding out domestic investment through a higher real rate.

**Explanation:** With r pinned by world markets, investment demand need not fall. The saving shortfall is financed by a larger capital inflow, so \(CA=S-I\) becomes more negative.


**Common trap:** This contrasts with the closed-economy crowding-out mechanism where lower S pushes r up and I down.


*Tags: small-open-economy, fiscal-policy, current-account*


### ch09-015 — Investment boom in small open economy · scenario · D2

**Q:** What happens if domestic investment demand rises in a small open economy while national saving and r* are unchanged?

**Answer:** Domestic investment rises and the current account moves toward deficit; net capital inflow finances the extra investment.

**Explanation:** The world rate prevents the domestic interest rate from needing to rise enough to force saving equal to investment.


**Common trap:** Do not interpret every current-account deficit as consumption beyond means; it can reflect high productive investment.


*Tags: small-open-economy, investment, current-account*


### ch09-016 — Nominal exchange rate convention · recall · D1

**Q:** What nominal exchange-rate convention is used for Australia in the course examples?

**Answer:** \(e\) is commonly units of foreign currency per Australian dollar, e.g. USD/AUD.

**Explanation:** If \(e=0.65 USD/AUD\), A$1 buys US$0.65. Under this quotation, a rise in e means the Australian dollar buys more foreign currency.


**Common trap:** Some textbooks quote the reciprocal. Always inspect the units before deciding whether a numerical rise means appreciation or depreciation.


*Tags: exchange-rate, notation, high-yield*


### ch09-017 — Appreciation · recall · D1

**Q:** Under the convention \(e\) = foreign currency per A$, what is an appreciation of the Australian dollar?

**Answer:** An increase in e: one Australian dollar buys more foreign currency.

**Explanation:** An appreciation makes foreign-currency goods/assets cheaper in AUD terms, all else equal, while making Australian goods more expensive to foreigners.


**Common trap:** If a question uses AUD per USD instead, the numerical direction reverses. Units rule everything.


*Tags: exchange-rate, appreciation*


### ch09-018 — Depreciation · recall · D1

**Q:** Under \(e\) = foreign currency per A$, what is a depreciation of the Australian dollar?

**Answer:** A decrease in e: one Australian dollar buys less foreign currency.

**Explanation:** Foreign goods become more expensive in AUD terms, while Australian goods become cheaper to foreigners, other things equal.


**Common trap:** Do not use “e rises = depreciation” unless the exchange rate is explicitly quoted as domestic currency per unit foreign currency.


*Tags: exchange-rate, depreciation*


### ch09-019 — Currency conversion · calculation · D1

**Q:** If \(e=0.65 USD/AUD\), how many USD does A$200 buy?

**Answer:** US$130.

**Explanation:** Multiply AUD by USD per AUD: \(200\times0.65=130\). Dimensional analysis cancels AUD and leaves USD.


**Common trap:** Do not divide when the quoted units already tell you USD obtained per one AUD.


*Tags: exchange-rate, calculation*


### ch09-020 — Currency conversion · calculation · D1

**Q:** If \(e=0.65 USD/AUD\), how many AUD are needed for US$130?

**Answer:** A$200.

**Explanation:** Divide USD by USD/AUD: \(130/0.65=200\) AUD. This is the reciprocal conversion of the previous card.


**Common trap:** Use units to decide multiply versus divide rather than memorising a rule.


*Tags: exchange-rate, calculation*


### ch09-021 — Cross rate · calculation · D2

**Q:** If 1 \(AUD=0.65 USD\) and 1 \(EUR=1.10 USD\), approximately how many EUR does 1 AUD buy?

**Answer:** \(0.65/1.10\approx0.591\) EUR.

**Explanation:** Convert the AUD’s 0.65 USD purchasing power into euros using 1 EUR per 1.10 USD. Cross rates are just consistent unit conversions.


**Common trap:** Make sure both quoted rates share a common currency in compatible directions before dividing.


*Tags: exchange-rate, cross-rate, calculation*


### ch09-022 — Real exchange rate · formula · D2

**Q:** With e quoted as foreign currency per domestic currency, what real exchange-rate formula is used?

**Answer:** \(q = e P_domestic / P_foreign\).

**Explanation:** q compares the foreign-currency price of the domestic consumption basket with the foreign basket. A rise in q is a real appreciation under this convention.


**Common trap:** If the nominal quote is inverted, the real-rate formula must be inverted too; do not mix conventions.


*Tags: real-exchange-rate, formula*


### ch09-023 — Real appreciation · recall · D2

**Q:** What does a real appreciation mean for relative competitiveness, all else equal?

**Answer:** Domestic goods become relatively more expensive than foreign goods, tending to reduce net exports.

**Explanation:** With \(q=eP/P^*\), q rises if the nominal currency appreciates or domestic prices rise relative to foreign prices. Foreigners find domestic goods dearer while residents find foreign goods relatively cheaper.


**Common trap:** A nominal depreciation can coexist with a real appreciation if domestic inflation is sufficiently higher than foreign inflation.


*Tags: real-exchange-rate, net-exports*


### ch09-024 — Law of one price · recall · D1

**Q:** What is the law of one price (LOOP)?

**Answer:** Identical tradable goods should sell for the same price in different locations once prices are expressed in a common currency, absent transport costs and trade barriers.

**Explanation:** Arbitrage tends to eliminate large risk-free price gaps: buyers purchase where cheap and sellers redirect goods toward where expensive.


**Common trap:** LOOP is a benchmark, not an exact empirical law when goods differ or arbitrage is costly.


*Tags: LOOP, PPP*


### ch09-025 — LOOP exchange rate · calculation · D1

**Q:** A Big Mac costs A$8 in Australia and US$5.20 in the US. What USD/AUD rate would equalise the common-currency price under LOOP?

**Answer:** \(5.20/8=0.65\) USD/AUD.

**Explanation:** At \(e=0.65\), the Australian burger costs 8×0.65=US$5.20. This is the Worksheet 8 style of calculation.


**Common trap:** For foreign currency per AUD, divide the foreign price by the Australian price.


*Tags: LOOP, exchange-rate, calculation*


### ch09-026 — Purchasing power parity · recall · D2

**Q:** What is purchasing power parity (PPP)?

**Answer:** The aggregate-price-level extension of the law of one price: exchange rates should adjust so comparable baskets have similar common-currency purchasing power.

**Explanation:** In the long run, countries with persistently higher inflation tend to experience nominal currency depreciation, other things equal. Relative PPP focuses on changes rather than exact equality of price levels.


**Common trap:** PPP is a long-run benchmark and performs poorly as an exact short-run exchange-rate model.


*Tags: PPP, exchange-rate*


### ch09-027 — PPP limitations · recall · D2

**Q:** Why can PPP fail substantially in practice?

**Answer:** Non-tradable goods, transport costs, tariffs, product differences, local distribution costs, market segmentation and slow price adjustment limit arbitrage.

**Explanation:** A haircut or rent cannot be cheaply shipped across borders, and even tradable retail prices embed local wages/rents/taxes. These wedges allow persistent common-currency price differences.


**Common trap:** A PPP deviation is not necessarily a free arbitrage opportunity.


*Tags: PPP, limitations*


### ch09-028 — Relative PPP · relationship · D2

**Q:** If Australian inflation persistently exceeds foreign inflation, what nominal exchange-rate movement does relative PPP predict for the AUD under foreign-currency-per-AUD notation?

**Answer:** Depreciation: e tends to fall.

**Explanation:** Higher Australian prices would otherwise make Australian goods increasingly expensive in common currency. A lower e offsets some of the inflation differential.


**Common trap:** Because e is foreign currency per AUD, depreciation is a fall, not a rise.


*Tags: PPP, inflation, exchange-rate*


### ch09-029 — AUD demand · recall · D1

**Q:** Who demands Australian dollars in the foreign-exchange market?

**Answer:** Foreigners who need AUD to buy Australian goods/services/assets, and anyone shifting portfolios toward AUD-denominated claims.

**Explanation:** Australian exports and foreign investment into Australia are major sources of AUD demand in the simplified market diagram.


**Common trap:** Australian residents importing foreign goods generally supply AUD and demand foreign currency, not demand AUD.


*Tags: FX-market, demand*


### ch09-030 — AUD supply · recall · D1

**Q:** Who supplies Australian dollars in the foreign-exchange market?

**Answer:** Australian residents/institutions exchanging AUD for foreign currency to buy foreign goods, services or assets.

**Explanation:** Imports and acquisitions of foreign assets generate AUD supply in the simplified FX market.


**Common trap:** Foreigners buying Australian exports generate demand for AUD, the opposite side.


*Tags: FX-market, supply*


### ch09-031 — FX shift · scenario · D1

**Q:** What happens to the AUD if foreign demand for Australian exports rises, other things equal?

**Answer:** Demand for AUD shifts right and the AUD appreciates.

**Explanation:** Foreign buyers need more AUD to pay Australian producers. At the old exchange rate there is excess demand for AUD, bidding up its foreign-currency price.


**Common trap:** Do not shift AUD supply because the physical goods are flowing out; the currency-market transaction is foreigners demanding AUD.


*Tags: FX-market, shift, exports*


### ch09-032 — FX shift · scenario · D1

**Q:** What happens to the AUD if Australians increase imports, other things equal?

**Answer:** Supply of AUD in the FX market shifts right and the AUD depreciates.

**Explanation:** Residents exchange more AUD for foreign currency to pay overseas sellers. At the old e there is excess AUD supply.


**Common trap:** Imports are demand for foreign currency and therefore supply of AUD in the bilateral simplification.


*Tags: FX-market, shift, imports*


### ch09-033 — Interest rates and FX · scenario · D2

**Q:** What is the usual short-run effect of a rise in Australian interest rates relative to foreign rates, all else equal?

**Answer:** Greater demand for AUD assets/net capital inflow tends to appreciate the AUD.

**Explanation:** Higher relative expected returns make Australian financial assets more attractive, raising AUD demand and/or reducing Australian demand for foreign assets.


**Common trap:** This relationship depends on expectations and risk; it is a standard ceteris-paribus exam mechanism, not a guaranteed daily-market prediction.


*Tags: FX-market, interest-rate, capital-flow*


### ch09-034 — Flexible exchange rate · recall · D1

**Q:** What determines a flexible/floating exchange rate in the simplified model?

**Answer:** The market-clearing intersection of currency demand and supply, with the rate moving in response to shifts.

**Explanation:** The central bank does not promise to convert domestic currency at a fixed official price. Market adjustments equilibrate desired purchases/sales.


**Common trap:** A floating central bank can still influence the exchange rate indirectly through interest rates or occasional intervention.


*Tags: exchange-rate-regime, floating*


### ch09-035 — Fixed exchange rate · recall · D2

**Q:** What is a fixed exchange-rate regime?

**Answer:** The central bank/government commits to maintain the currency at or near an announced rate by intervening and using policy/reserves as needed.

**Explanation:** At the peg, private FX demand and supply need not balance, so the central bank becomes the residual buyer or seller of domestic currency.


**Common trap:** Announcing a peg does not make market pressure disappear; it changes who must absorb the imbalance.


*Tags: exchange-rate-regime, fixed*


### ch09-036 — Overvalued peg · scenario · D2

**Q:** If a fixed AUD rate is above its market-clearing value under the foreign-currency-per-AUD convention, what imbalance exists and how must the central bank defend it?

**Answer:** The AUD is overvalued with excess supply; the central bank must buy AUD and sell foreign reserves.

**Explanation:** Private agents want to sell more AUD than foreigners want at the artificially high price. The central bank absorbs the excess AUD, paying out foreign currency from reserves.


**Common trap:** To support an overvalued domestic currency, the central bank spends foreign reserves; it does not sell more domestic currency.


*Tags: fixed-exchange-rate, reserves, high-yield*


### ch09-037 — Undervalued peg · scenario · D2

**Q:** If a fixed AUD rate is below market-clearing value, what must the central bank do?

**Answer:** There is excess demand for AUD; the central bank supplies/sells AUD and acquires foreign reserves.

**Explanation:** The peg makes AUD artificially cheap. The central bank satisfies excess private demand by issuing/selling domestic currency in exchange for foreign assets.


**Common trap:** This intervention tends to accumulate, not deplete, foreign reserves.


*Tags: fixed-exchange-rate, reserves*


### ch09-038 — Speculative attack · recall · D2

**Q:** What is a speculative attack on a fixed exchange rate?

**Answer:** Large-scale selling of a currency when market participants expect the central bank cannot or will not maintain the peg.

**Explanation:** If the currency is overvalued, speculators may exchange domestic currency for the central bank’s scarce foreign reserves before an expected devaluation. This can accelerate reserve loss.


**Common trap:** The attack is tied to expectations about peg sustainability, not merely ordinary day-to-day currency trading.


*Tags: fixed-exchange-rate, speculative-attack*


### ch09-039 — Defending a peg · scenario · D3

**Q:** What policy choices can a central bank facing an overvalued peg/speculative attack make?

**Answer:** Use foreign reserves, raise interest rates/tighten policy to support currency demand, impose controls in some regimes, or eventually devalue/abandon the peg.

**Explanation:** Higher rates can attract/retain capital but may impose a domestic recession cost. Reserves are finite, making an inconsistent peg vulnerable.


**Common trap:** There is no unlimited ability to sell foreign reserves; sustainability depends on reserve stocks and policy credibility.


*Tags: fixed-exchange-rate, speculative-attack, policy*


### ch09-040 — Monetary transmission via FX · sequence · D2

**Q:** How can tighter Australian monetary policy reduce aggregate demand through the exchange-rate channel?

**Answer:** Australian rates ↑ → AUD assets become relatively attractive → AUD appreciates → exports become less competitive/imports cheaper → net exports ↓ → AD ↓.

**Explanation:** This channel reinforces the domestic interest-rate effects on C and I in an open economy.


**Common trap:** An appreciation normally reduces NX under the course convention; do not confuse it with the benefit consumers get from cheaper imports.


*Tags: monetary-policy, exchange-rate, net-exports*


## Chapter 10: Economic Growth (30 cards)


### ch10-001 — Economic growth · recall · D1

**Q:** What measure is most closely tied to long-run improvement in average material living standards?

**Answer:** Growth in real GDP per capita.

**Explanation:** Real GDP removes price changes; dividing by population distinguishes a larger economy from higher average output/income per person. Broader welfare measures can still differ from GDP per capita.


**Common trap:** Total real GDP can rise while real GDP per capita falls if population grows faster.


*Tags: growth, GDP-per-capita*


### ch10-002 — Growth rate · formula · D1

**Q:** How is a one-period growth rate calculated?

**Answer:** \(g = \frac{Y_t-Y_{t-1}}{Y_{t-1}}\), usually ×100% for a percentage.

**Explanation:** Growth compares the change with the initial/base-period level. Repeated growth compounds rather than adding the same absolute amount each year.


**Common trap:** The denominator is the earlier value, not the later value.


*Tags: growth, formula*


### ch10-003 — Compound growth · formula · D1

**Q:** If output grows at a constant rate g for n periods, what is its future level?

**Answer:** \(Y_n = Y_0(1+g)^n\).

**Explanation:** Each period’s growth applies to the enlarged previous-period base. This compounding makes small persistent growth-rate differences produce huge long-run level differences.


**Common trap:** For 3%, use 1.03, not 1+3.


*Tags: growth, compound-growth, formula*


### ch10-004 — Rule of 70 · recall · D1

**Q:** What is the rule of 70 (or roughly 69/72) for growth?

**Answer:** Doubling time in years \(\approx\frac{70}{\text{annual percentage growth rate}}\).

**Explanation:** At 2% annual growth, a variable doubles in about 35 years; at 3.5%, about 20 years. The rule approximates compound growth using logarithms.


**Common trap:** Put the percentage number in the denominator: use 2, not 0.02.


*Tags: growth, rule-of-70*


### ch10-005 — Rule of 70 · calculation · D1

**Q:** At 2.5% annual real GDP per capita growth, about how long does living-standard output take to double?

**Answer:** About 28 years.

**Explanation:** 70/2.5=28. The exact compound-growth answer is close. Such calculations highlight the power of persistent differences in growth rates.


**Common trap:** Do not divide 70 by 0.025.


*Tags: growth, calculation*


### ch10-006 — Growth-rate differences · recall · D2

**Q:** Why do seemingly small differences in long-run growth rates matter so much?

**Answer:** Because they compound over many years, creating very large differences in future income levels.

**Explanation:** An economy growing 3% rather than 2% does not merely gain one extra percentage point once; the increasingly larger base itself continues to grow.


**Common trap:** Do not compare long-run outcomes by multiplying the annual percentage-point gap by years without compounding.


*Tags: growth, compound-growth*


### ch10-007 — Convergence · recall · D2

**Q:** What is economic convergence/catch-up growth?

**Answer:** The tendency for poorer economies to grow faster than richer economies when they have access to similar technology and sufficiently supportive institutions/conditions.

**Explanation:** With low capital per worker, marginal returns to additional capital can be high, and poorer countries can adopt existing technologies rather than inventing everything at the frontier.


**Common trap:** Poverty alone does not guarantee convergence; weak institutions, human capital, conflict or technology access can prevent catch-up.


*Tags: growth, convergence*


### ch10-008 — Production function · recall · D1

**Q:** What does an aggregate production function describe?

**Answer:** How an economy’s productive inputs and productivity/technology combine to produce real output.

**Explanation:** A generic form is \(Y=F(K,L,A)\), where K is capital, L labour and A captures productivity/technology. It is a supply-side relationship distinct from the short-run expenditure determination of output.


**Common trap:** Do not treat more aggregate demand as an input that permanently raises potential output in a production function.


*Tags: production-function, growth*


### ch10-009 — Cobb-Douglas · formula · D1

**Q:** State the Cobb-Douglas aggregate production function used in the course.

**Answer:** \(Y = A K^\alpha L^{1-\alpha}\), with \(0<\alpha<1\).

**Explanation:** A measures total factor productivity; \(\alpha\) is capital’s exponent/output elasticity and \(1-\alpha\) labour’s. This functional form has positive but diminishing marginal products and constant returns to scale.


**Common trap:** The exponents sum to one in this course version.


*Tags: Cobb-Douglas, formula*


### ch10-010 — Constant returns to scale · recall · D2

**Q:** What does constant returns to scale mean for \(Y = A K^\alpha L^{1-\alpha}\)?

**Answer:** If K and L are both multiplied by the same factor x, output is multiplied by x.

**Explanation:** Because the exponents sum to one: \((xK)^\alpha(xL)^{1-\alpha}=xK^\alpha L^{1-\alpha}\). Doubling both inputs doubles output with A unchanged.


**Common trap:** Constant returns to scale is about changing all reproducible inputs together, not the marginal effect of changing K alone.


*Tags: Cobb-Douglas, returns-to-scale*


### ch10-011 — Diminishing marginal product · recall · D1

**Q:** What does diminishing marginal product of capital mean?

**Answer:** Holding labour and productivity fixed, each additional unit of capital raises output by less than the previous one.

**Explanation:** When workers already have abundant machines/infrastructure, adding another unit is less transformative than when capital is scarce. Cobb-Douglas with \(0<\alpha<1\) has this property.


**Common trap:** Diminishing marginal product does not mean total output falls when capital rises; MPK remains positive.


*Tags: marginal-product, capital*


### ch10-012 — Diminishing marginal product · scenario · D2

**Q:** After a disaster destroys much of a country’s capital stock while labour and technology are initially unchanged, what happens to the marginal product of remaining/new capital?

**Answer:** It rises.

**Explanation:** Capital has become scarcer relative to labour. Under diminishing MPK, moving left to a lower K raises the extra output generated by an additional unit of capital, supporting rapid reconstruction/catch-up if institutions and financing permit.


**Common trap:** The level of output falls after the destruction even though the marginal return to rebuilding capital rises.


*Tags: marginal-product, capital, scenario*


### ch10-013 — Marginal product of labour · recall · D2

**Q:** What is the marginal product of labour (MPL) in the aggregate production function?

**Answer:** The extra output produced by an additional unit of labour, holding capital and productivity fixed.

**Explanation:** As in Chapter 2’s labour-demand model, firms value labour according to the additional production it generates. More capital or better technology can raise labour productivity/MPL.


**Common trap:** Average labour productivity \(Y/L\) and \(MPL\) are related but not generally identical.


*Tags: marginal-product, labour*


### ch10-014 — Output per worker · formula · D1

**Q:** Under Cobb-Douglas and constant returns, what is output per worker?

**Answer:** \(y = \frac{Y}{L} = A\left(\frac{K}{L}\right)^\alpha = A k^\alpha\).

**Explanation:** Define \(k=K/L\). Living-standard productivity depends on capital per worker and total factor productivity, not merely the economy’s total capital stock.


**Common trap:** Increasing \(K\) and \(L\) at the same rate raises total \(Y\) but leaves \(K/L\) unchanged; without \(A\) growth it need not raise output per worker.


*Tags: per-worker, Cobb-Douglas, formula*


### ch10-015 — Capital deepening · recall · D2

**Q:** What is capital deepening?

**Answer:** An increase in capital per worker, \(K/L\).

**Explanation:** More or better equipment/infrastructure per worker raises labour productivity, but diminishing returns mean capital deepening alone cannot sustain a constant positive growth rate of output per worker forever without productivity growth.


**Common trap:** A larger total K caused only by a proportionally larger workforce is not capital deepening.


*Tags: capital-deepening, growth*


### ch10-016 — Capital accumulation limits · recall · D2

**Q:** Why can’t simply accumulating physical capital generate permanently high per-capita growth on its own in the standard model?

**Answer:** Because diminishing marginal returns make each further increase in capital per worker contribute less to output per worker.

**Explanation:** Capital accumulation can generate transitional/catch-up growth, especially from a low starting point, but sustained frontier growth ultimately requires ongoing productivity/technology improvement.


**Common trap:** This does not mean investment is unimportant; it means investment alone faces diminishing returns.


*Tags: capital, diminishing-returns, growth*


### ch10-017 — Types of capital · recall · D1

**Q:** What broad forms of capital can support economic growth beyond private physical machinery?

**Answer:** Physical/private capital, public infrastructure and human capital such as education, skills and health.

**Explanation:** Roads, communications and institutions can complement private production; human capital raises workers’ productive capabilities. The chapter treats growth as broader than merely purchasing machines.


**Common trap:** Human capital is productive knowledge/skills embodied in people, not the same thing as financial wealth.


*Tags: capital, human-capital, public-capital*


### ch10-018 — Productivity · recall · D2

**Q:** What is total factor productivity (TFP), represented by A in Cobb-Douglas?

**Answer:** The efficiency with which measured capital and labour are transformed into output.

**Explanation:** A rises with better technology, organisation, knowledge, institutions and other efficiency improvements not captured simply by quantities of K and L. It is central to sustained long-run growth.


**Common trap:** TFP is not a directly observed physical input; growth accounting commonly measures its growth as a residual.


*Tags: TFP, productivity, growth*


### ch10-019 — Technology and ideas · recall · D2

**Q:** Why are technological ideas especially important for sustained growth?

**Answer:** Ideas can improve productivity repeatedly and are often non-rival: one person’s use of knowledge need not prevent others using it.

**Explanation:** Unlike a machine, a formula or design can potentially be replicated at low marginal cost, creating increasing social returns to innovation and knowledge diffusion.


**Common trap:** Non-rival does not necessarily mean non-excludable; patents, secrecy and licensing can restrict access.


*Tags: technology, ideas, growth*


### ch10-020 — Public-good properties · contrast · D1

**Q:** What do non-rival and non-excludable mean?

**Answer:** Non-rival: one person’s use does not materially reduce availability to others. Non-excludable: it is difficult/costly to prevent non-payers from using it.

**Explanation:** Pure public goods have both properties. Knowledge is often highly non-rival but can be made partly excludable through intellectual-property rights or secrecy.


**Common trap:** Do not treat the two properties as synonyms.


*Tags: public-good, definition*


### ch10-021 — Innovation incentives · recall · D3

**Q:** What tension does intellectual-property protection address in the growth context?

**Answer:** It trades off incentives to create costly new ideas against broad diffusion/use of ideas once created.

**Explanation:** Temporary exclusivity can let innovators recover research costs, while overly strong protection can slow imitation and follow-on innovation. The socially efficient balance is not necessarily zero or infinite protection.


**Common trap:** Because knowledge is non-rival, the marginal social cost of another user can be very low even when invention was expensive.


*Tags: technology, intellectual-property*


### ch10-022 — Institutions and property rights · recall · D2

**Q:** Why can secure property rights and effective institutions raise long-run growth?

**Answer:** They improve incentives to invest, innovate, contract and allocate resources by making returns more predictable and reducing expropriation/transaction costs.

**Explanation:** Even high-return technologies may not be adopted if firms expect assets to be seized or contracts unenforced. Institutions therefore influence both capital accumulation and productivity.


**Common trap:** Institutions are not merely an accounting input like K; they affect incentives and efficiency throughout the economy.


*Tags: institutions, property-rights, growth*


### ch10-023 — Social capital · recall · D2

**Q:** What is social capital in a growth context?

**Answer:** Trust, norms and networks that make cooperation, exchange and institutions work more effectively.

**Explanation:** Greater trust can lower contracting and monitoring costs and facilitate investment/knowledge sharing. It is difficult to measure but can help explain productivity differences not captured by physical inputs.


**Common trap:** Social capital here does not mean the dollar value of social-media companies.


*Tags: social-capital, institutions*


### ch10-024 — Natural capital · recall · D2

**Q:** How can natural capital matter for economic growth and welfare?

**Answer:** Natural resources and environmental systems provide productive inputs and services; depletion or degradation can raise measured output today while reducing future productive/welfare capacity.

**Explanation:** A growth strategy that ignores resource sustainability can overstate durable gains. The textbook includes natural capital among broader determinants of productive capacity.


**Common trap:** GDP records current production flows and does not automatically subtract every loss of natural wealth.


*Tags: natural-capital, growth, welfare*


### ch10-025 — Growth accounting · formula · D2

**Q:** What is the Cobb-Douglas growth-accounting decomposition?

**Answer:** \(g_Y \approx g_A + \alpha g_K + (1-\alpha)g_L\).

**Explanation:** Output growth is decomposed into TFP growth plus input growth weighted by their output elasticities/income shares. The residual after accounting for K and L is measured productivity growth.


**Common trap:** Do not simply add raw K and L growth rates without applying the \(\alpha\) and \(1-\alpha\) weights.


*Tags: growth-accounting, formula*


### ch10-026 — Growth accounting · calculation · D1

**Q:** If output grows 3.5%, K grows 2%, L grows 2%, and \(\alpha=0.25\), what is TFP growth?

**Answer:** 1.5%.

**Explanation:** Weighted input growth is 0.25(2%)+0.75(2%)=2%. Therefore \(g_A=3.5\%-2\%=1.5\%\). This matches the tutorial’s worked style.


**Common trap:** When K and L grow at the same rate, their weights sum to one, so their combined contribution equals that common rate.


*Tags: growth-accounting, calculation*


### ch10-027 — Growth accounting · calculation · D2

**Q:** If \(\alpha=0.3\), K grows 5%, L grows 1% and TFP grows 2%, what is predicted output growth?

**Answer:** 2% + 0.3(5%) + 0.7(1%) = 4.2%.

**Explanation:** Capital contributes 1.5 percentage points, labour 0.7 and TFP 2, summing to 4.2.


**Common trap:** These are percentage-point contributions to output growth, not shares of the final 4.2 that need renormalising.


*Tags: growth-accounting, calculation*


### ch10-028 — Labour productivity · formula · D3

**Q:** How can real GDP per capita be decomposed into labour productivity and employment intensity?

**Answer:** \(Y/\text{Population}=(Y/L)\times(L/\text{Population})\).

**Explanation:** Average living-standard output rises if output per worker rises or if a larger share of the population works, holding the other factor fixed. Long-run sustained gains are especially tied to productivity.


**Common trap:** A rise in employment/population can raise GDP per capita without increasing output per worker.


*Tags: GDP-per-capita, labour-productivity, formula*


### ch10-029 — Catch-up versus frontier growth · contrast · D3

**Q:** Why can a capital-poor country grow rapidly without inventing frontier technology, while a rich frontier economy depends more on innovation?

**Answer:** The poorer country can earn high returns from capital deepening and adopt existing technologies; a frontier economy has less easy imitation/catch-up available and faces stronger diminishing returns to further capital accumulation.

**Explanation:** Convergence is therefore partly a transitional process, while sustained global-frontier growth requires continued productivity/idea creation.


**Common trap:** Catch-up growth still needs institutions, skills and access that allow technology adoption.


*Tags: convergence, technology, capital*


### ch10-030 — Growth policy · scenario · D3

**Q:** Which policy is more likely to raise the long-run level of output but face diminishing returns if used alone: permanently subsidising physical capital accumulation or permanently improving productivity growth?

**Answer:** Physical-capital accumulation alone mainly raises the level/transitional growth and faces diminishing returns; sustained productivity growth can support ongoing per-capita growth.

**Explanation:** This distinction is central to Chapter 10. A one-off productivity-level improvement also mainly raises the level, whereas continued technological progress sustains a growth rate.


**Common trap:** Be precise about “level of A” versus “growth rate of A”; only ongoing productivity improvement sustains ongoing productivity growth.


*Tags: growth-policy, capital, TFP*

### mix-005 — Investment versus saving · mcq · D1

**Q:** Which action is macroeconomic investment rather than household saving/portfolio reallocation?

**Explanation:** Macroeconomic investment means creation of new capital, housing or inventories. Buying shares reallocates ownership of financial claims; saving is income not consumed.


**Common trap:** Finance uses “investment” to mean buying assets, but national accounts use a narrower production-of-capital definition.

### mix-006 — Crowding out · mcq · D2

**Q:** In the closed-economy saving-investment model, a larger government deficit reduces national saving. What is the standard sequence?

**Answer:** Saving supply shifts left → real interest rate rises → private investment falls.

**Explanation:** The real rate adjusts to restore \(S=I\). The fall in private investment is called crowding out.


**Common trap:** This exact mechanism changes in the small-open-economy model where r is pinned by the world rate.


**Choices:** A. Saving right → r falls → I rises | B. Saving left → r rises → I falls | C. Investment right → r rises → saving falls | D. Investment left → r falls → saving falls

### mix-007 — PAE equilibrium · mcq · D1

**Q:** At the current output level, planned aggregate expenditure is less than actual output. What happens to inventories and production?

**Answer:** Unplanned inventories accumulate, leading firms to reduce production/output.

**Explanation:** Firms produced more than customers planned to buy. The unsold goods are unplanned inventory investment, signalling firms to cut future production until \(Y=PAE\).


**Common trap:** Inventory accumulation is the adjustment signal, not evidence that planned investment intentionally increased.


**Choices:** A. Inventories fall; firms cut output | B. Inventories rise; firms cut output | C. Inventories fall; firms raise output | D. Inventories rise; firms raise output
**Correct:** B

### mix-008 — Multiplier · mcq · D1

**Q:** In a two-sector model with \(MPC=0.75\), autonomous investment rises by $20. By how much does equilibrium GDP rise?

**Answer:** $80.

**Explanation:** The multiplier is \(\frac{1}{1-0.75}=4\), so \(\Delta Y=4\times 20=80\).


**Common trap:** Do not report the first-round $20 change only.


**Choices:** A. $15 | B. $20 | C. $60 | D. $80

### mix-009 — Fiscal multiplier · mcq · D2

**Q:** With \(c=0.8\) and proportional tax rate \(t=0.25\), what is the government-spending multiplier?

### mix-010 — Balanced budget · mcq · D2

**Q:** If government spending and autonomous taxes both rise by the same amount, which statement is generally correct in the course model with proportional taxes?

**Answer:** Equilibrium GDP rises, but the balanced-budget multiplier need not equal one.

### mix-021 — Current account · mcq · D1

**Q:** A small open economy has national saving of 100 and domestic investment of 130. What is its current account?

**Answer:** -30, a deficit.

**Explanation:** \(CA=S-I=100-130=-30\). The economy finances the excess of domestic investment over national saving with net foreign capital inflow.


**Common trap:** Do not reverse the identity to \(I-S\).


**Choices:** A. +230 | B. +30 | C. -30 | D. -230
**Correct:** C

### ch06-032 — Money destruction · sequence · D2

**Q:** In the simplified bank-balance-sheet model, what happens to deposit money when a borrower repays the principal of a bank loan from a deposit account at that bank?

**Answer:** The bank loan asset and the matching deposit liability both fall by the principal repaid, so the corresponding deposit money is destroyed.

**Explanation:** New bank lending can create a loan asset and a deposit liability together. Repayment reverses that entry: the borrower’s deposit is used to reduce the loan principal, lowering both the bank’s loan asset and its deposit liability. A loan default or write-off can also reduce the bank asset and equity, but the exact deposit effect depends on how the loss is settled; do not treat every bank loss as identical to a voluntary repayment.

**Common trap:** An interbank payment normally transfers deposits and reserves between banks; it does not by itself destroy total deposit money. Principal repayment is the clean reverse of loan-created deposit money. A loan write-off primarily reduces the loan asset and bank equity; it does not automatically destroy an equal deposit balance.

**Tags:** chapter6, money, banking, reverse-mechanism


### ch07-029 — Cash rate and short-term securities · sequence · D2

**Q:** In the course model, how can a higher RBA cash-rate target affect an existing short-term government security?

**Answer:** It raises the relevant required/expected short-term return; demand for the existing fixed-payment security falls, so its price falls and its yield rises.

**Explanation:** The cash rate is an overnight rate, so the expectations and term-structure link matters. Holding the security’s promised payments fixed, a higher market return makes the existing security less attractive at its old price. Buyers therefore require a lower price, which raises the security’s yield. The RBA’s own OMO changes ES balances and is a separate market operation; do not confuse that reserve-market supply shift with the price adjustment of an already-issued bond or Treasury bill.

**Common trap:** A higher required return does not raise the price of an existing fixed-payment security. Price and yield move in opposite directions.

**Tags:** chapter7, monetary, cash-rate, bond, mechanism


### ch09-041 — Trade-weighted index · scenario · D2

**Q:** What does a sustained rise in the real trade-weighted index usually indicate in the course model?

**Answer:** A real appreciation of the domestic currency against its weighted trading-partner basket, making domestic goods relatively more expensive and tending to reduce net exports.

**Explanation:** A trade-weighted index combines exchange-rate movements against important trading partners rather than looking at only one bilateral rate. The real version also reflects relative price levels. Under the course convention, a rise indicates that domestic goods have become more expensive relative to foreign goods on average, reducing export competitiveness and encouraging imports, other things equal.

**Common trap:** Do not read a TWI as a simple nominal AUD/USD quote or as a direct measure of export volume. Check whether the index is nominal or real before interpreting inflation effects.
